# Robert A. Brown: An Exhaustive Scientific Biography

## Overview

Robert Arthur Brown (born July 22, 1951, in San Antonio, Texas) is an American chemical engineer, applied mathematician, and university administrator whose career spans four decades of research in computational transport phenomena and materials processing, followed by two decades of academic leadership at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and Boston University (BU). He is internationally recognized as a pioneer in the use of finite-element methods and numerical continuation techniques to solve coupled fluid mechanics, heat transfer, and mass transfer problems arising in crystal growth and polymer processing. He served as the 10th President of Boston University (2005–2023) after a 25-year career on the MIT faculty, culminating in the role of Provost (1998–2005).

## 2. Research Career and Scientific Contributions

Brown's research program, developed principally at MIT from 1979 onward, centered on **mathematical modeling, numerical simulation, and stability analysis of transport processes in materials with evolving or complex microstructure.** He authored or co-authored more than 250 peer-reviewed papers. His work is generally organized around two major thrusts:

### 2.1 Crystal Growth and Semiconductor Materials Processing

Brown became one of the leading theoreticians of **melt crystal growth**, developing finite-element-based computational models to describe the coupled convection, heat transfer, and mass transfer occurring during the solidification of single-crystal materials — most notably silicon grown by the **Czochralski (CZ) method**, used industrially to produce semiconductor wafers.

Key contributions include:
- Development of efficient finite-element algorithms for calculating **buoyancy-driven convection and melt/solid interface shape** in directional solidification systems (with C. J. Chang and others), enabling quantitative prediction of dopant and impurity distribution in grown crystals.
- Detailed simulation of **point-defect dynamics** (vacancies and self-interstitials) during Czochralski silicon growth, establishing relationships between crystal pull rate (*V*) and axial temperature gradient (*G*) that determine whether crystals form voids, stacking faults, or achieve near-perfect crystallinity — work with direct industrial relevance to semiconductor wafer quality.
- Studies of **oxygen-related defects** in silicon, including how microscopic spatial inhomogeneities in oxygen concentration (arising from growth-related fluctuations) enhance effective nucleation rates, and how dissolved oxygen impedes dislocation motion under applied stress.
- Broader theoretical work on the **shapes and stability of fluid interfaces**, extending the three-dimensional interface problems first explored in his doctoral thesis to industrially relevant melt-growth geometries.

This body of work established rigorous, physically grounded computational tools that influenced both academic understanding and industrial practice in semiconductor crystal manufacturing.

### 2.2 Polymer Fluid Mechanics and Viscoelastic Flow

Brown's second major research thrust concerned the **rheology and fluid mechanics of polymeric (viscoelastic) liquids**, an area central to plastics processing, fiber spinning, extrusion, and coating operations.

Key contributions include:
- Development of advanced **finite-element discretizations** (e.g., the DEVSS-G/discontinuous Galerkin, DEVSS-G/DG, method) for solving viscoelastic flow problems governed by nonlinear constitutive equations (such as the Giesekus model), including highly parallel time-integration schemes that decouple polymeric stress evolution from velocity-field computation.
- Numerical and experimental studies of **flow bifurcations and instabilities** in viscoelastic fluids, such as global viscoelastic flow transitions in planar contraction flows, characterized using light-sheet visualization and laser Doppler velocimetry.
- Application of **kinetic-theory-based micro-mechanical models** alongside continuum-level constitutive descriptions to connect polymer molecular configuration to bulk rheological behavior and flow-induced microstructure.
- Prediction of **steady-state flow behaviors and transitions** critical to industrial processes including extrusion, fiber spinning, and coating, with direct implications for manufacturing efficiency in plastics and textile industries.

### 2.3 Broader Methodological Contributions

Across both research thrusts, Brown was a leading contributor to the development and application of:
- **Finite-element numerical methods** for coupled, nonlinear, multi-physics transport problems.
- **Numerical continuation and bifurcation analysis** techniques for identifying and tracking instabilities and multiplicity of steady states in fluid systems.
- **Multi-scale modeling** frameworks connecting molecular/microstructural physics to macroscopic material and process behavior.

He served as **Executive Editor of *Chemical Engineering Science*** (1991–2004), one of the field's leading journals, and sat on the **Editorial Board of the *Journal of Non-Newtonian Fluid Mechanics*** (1993–2005), reflecting his standing in both the broader chemical engineering and specialized rheology/fluid-mechanics communities.

As **Head of the Department of Chemical Engineering** and later **Dean of Engineering**, Brown was credited with fostering interdisciplinary initiatives at MIT, including the creation of the **Biological Engineering Division** and the **Engineering Systems Division** as new cross-cutting units spanning teaching and research. As **Provost** — MIT's chief academic officer — he oversaw Institute-wide academic policy, budget, and faculty affairs from 1998 until his departure in 2005.

### 3.2 Boston University (2005–2023)

Brown was selected as the **10th President of Boston University** in May 2005 and inaugurated in September 2005, succeeding Aram V. Chobanian. He also held a faculty appointment as **Professor of Engineering** at BU throughout his presidency and continues as **Professor of Engineering and of Computing & Data Sciences** in his post-presidential (President Emeritus) role.

Notable elements of his presidency (administrative/institutional, distinct from his personal research record):
- Initiated an 18-month strategic planning process resulting in the 10-year plan *"Choosing to be Great"* (his first years in office).
- Led Boston University's admission to the **Association of American Universities (AAU)** in 2012, the consortium of leading North American research universities.
- Oversaw BU's first university-wide comprehensive fundraising campaign (launched 2012; goal raised from $1 billion to $1.5 billion in 2015).
- Established major academic units and facilities, including the **Arvind and Chandan Nandlal Kilachand Honors College** (dedicated 2011), the **Frederick S. Pardee School of Global Studies** (2014), the **Initiative on Cities** (2013, with former Boston Mayor Thomas Menino), the **National Emerging Infectious Diseases Laboratory (NEIDL)** (a BSL-4 facility), the **Rajen Kilachand Center for Integrated Life Sciences & Engineering**, and the **Center for Computing & Data Sciences** (a 19-story, carbon-free building supporting a university-wide initiative in computing, machine learning, and AI).
- Retired as BU President on July 31, 2023, succeeded on an interim basis by Kenneth W. Freeman and subsequently by Melissa L. Gilliam.

## 8. Summary Assessment

Robert A. Brown's scientific legacy rests on his role in establishing rigorous, computationally intensive approaches — chiefly finite-element analysis coupled with stability and bifurcation theory — as standard tools for understanding transport phenomena in two industrially critical domains: **melt crystal growth of semiconductor materials** and **viscoelastic polymer processing flows**. His NAE citation ("application of computing techniques to fundamental and practical problems in fluid mechanics, rheology, and crystal growth") and his 2014 Simon Ramo Founders Award capture the throughline of a research career that bridged applied mathematics, continuum physics, and materials manufacturing. This scientific foundation, combined with sustained departmental and institutional leadership at MIT, positioned him for a second, parallel career as a university president, during which he applied a similarly systematic, metrics-driven approach — strategic planning, benchmarking, and periodic review — to institutional transformation at Boston University.
