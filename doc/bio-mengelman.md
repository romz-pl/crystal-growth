# Michael S. Engelman: A Scientific Biography

## Overview

Michael S. Engelman is a computational fluid dynamicist and applied mathematician best known as the principal developer of FIDAP (Fluid Dynamics Analysis Program) and as co-founder, with mathematician Simon Rosenblat, of Fluid Dynamics International (FDI), a pioneering commercial computational fluid dynamics (CFD) software company founded in Evanston, Illinois, in the early-to-mid 1980s. Engelman's technical work sits at the foundation of the modern finite-element approach to incompressible flow simulation, and FIDAP was among the first general-purpose finite-element CFD codes to be commercialized, placing Engelman alongside figures such as Brian Spalding (CHAM/PHOENICS), Bart Patel (Creare/Fluent), and David Gosman (CD/STAR-CD) as one of the founding figures of the commercial CFD software industry.

## Education

- **B.Sc., Applied Mathematics** — University of Sydney, Australia (c. 1970–1973)
- **M.Sc., Applied Mathematics** — The Hebrew University of Jerusalem (c. 1974–1975)
- **Ph.D., Applied Mathematics** — The Hebrew University of Jerusalem (c. 1976–1979)

Engelman's doctoral training in applied mathematics gave him a strong grounding in numerical analysis and variational methods, which he subsequently applied to the emerging field of computational fluid dynamics — at the time a young discipline still establishing its mathematical foundations, particularly around the treatment of incompressibility constraints in the Navier–Stokes equations.

## Early Research Career

Following his doctorate, Engelman moved into fluid dynamics research in the United States, working within the CIRES (Cooperative Institute for Research in Environmental Sciences) program, a joint NOAA–University of Colorado Boulder research institute. It was during this period, in the late 1970s and early 1980s, that Engelman became associated with a loose but highly influential network of researchers working on finite-element methods for incompressible viscous flow — including Philip M. Gresho and Robert L. Lee (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory), Robert L. Sani (University of Colorado), and David F. Griffiths (University of Dundee).

This collaboration produced some of the most cited and foundational papers in the finite-element CFD literature:

- **Engelman, M.S. (1979).** *A finite element for the numerical solution of viscous incompressible flows.* Journal of Computational Physics. Introduced a penalty-type isoparametric finite element for incompressible Navier–Stokes solutions, comparing nine-node and eight-node quadrilateral elements.
- **Engelman, M.S. (1980).** *A finite-element method for incompressible non-Newtonian flows.* Journal of Computational Physics. Extended the penalty finite-element approach to non-Newtonian (including Bingham) fluids.
- **Sani, R.L., Gresho, P.M., Lee, R.L., Griffiths, D.F., and Engelman, M. (1981).** *The cause and cure(?) of the spurious pressures generated by certain FEM solutions of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations,* Parts 1 and 2. *International Journal for Numerical Methods in Fluids*, 1: 17–43 and 171–204. This two-part paper is regarded as a landmark contribution to the theory of mixed and penalty finite-element formulations for incompressible flow. It rigorously explained the origin of spurious ("checkerboard") pressure modes in certain velocity–pressure element pairings, introduced the taxonomy of "physical vs. spurious" and "pure vs. impure" pressure modes, and proposed pressure-filtering schemes to recover meaningful pressure fields from otherwise polluted solutions. The paper remains widely cited in the numerical analysis and CFD literature as one of the clearest expositions of the inf-sup (LBB) compatibility problem in practical, engineering terms.
- **Engelman, M.S., Sani, R.L., Gresho, P.M., and Bercovier, M. (1982).** *Consistent vs. reduced integration penalty methods for incompressible media using several old and new elements.* International Journal for Numerical Methods in Fluids, 2: 25–42. Compared reduced-integration and consistent penalty formulations and proposed new stable elements.
- **Engelman, M.S., and Sani, R.L. (1983).** *Finite-element simulation of an in-package pasteurization process.* Numerical Heat Transfer, 8: 41–54. An early applied/industrial demonstration of coupled flow–heat-transfer finite-element simulation.
- Contributions to the GAMM Workshop benchmark problem on flow past a backward-facing step (1983), an early and influential community benchmark for validating incompressible Navier–Stokes solvers.

This body of work fed directly into the two-volume reference text co-authored with his collaborators, **Gresho, P.M., Sani, R.L., and Engelman, M.S., *Incompressible Flow and the Finite Element Method*** (Advection–Diffusion and Isothermal Laminar Flow), John Wiley & Sons — a text that became a standard graduate-level reference on the finite element method applied to incompressible viscous flow.

## Academic Appointment

Engelman held a faculty appointment as **Assistant Professor of Applied Mathematics at the Illinois Institute of Technology (IIT)** in Chicago in the early 1980s. It was from this academic base in the Chicago area that he moved into commercial software development, translating the finite-element research program developed with Gresho, Sani, and colleagues into a production engineering analysis code.

## Founding of Fluid Dynamics International (FDI) and FIDAP

In the early-to-mid 1980s, Engelman co-founded **Fluid Dynamics International (FDI)** in Evanston, Illinois, together with mathematician **Simon Rosenblat**. FDI's flagship — and effectively sole — product was **FIDAP** (**F**lu**I**d **D**ynamics **A**nalysis **P**rogram), a general-purpose finite-element code for simulating steady-state and transient two-dimensional, axisymmetric, and three-dimensional incompressible fluid flows in complex geometries, including coupled heat transfer.

Engelman was the principal architect and primary developer of FIDAP, and the code was a direct commercial and engineering realization of the finite-element methodology he had helped establish in the academic literature — most notably the penalty-based treatment of incompressibility and the pressure-filtering and element-stability results from the Sani–Gresho–Lee–Griffiths–Engelman papers. Key published descriptions of the code include:

- **Engelman, M.S. (1982).** *FIDAP (A Fluid Dynamics Analysis Program).* Advances in Engineering Software, describing FIDAP as a general-purpose finite-element code for isothermal Newtonian and non-Newtonian flows, natural and forced convection, and atmospheric flow problems.
- Successive **FIDAP Theoretical Manuals** (e.g., Revision 4.0, 1987; Revision 6.0, 1991), published by FDI in Evanston, which documented the underlying numerical formulations, element library, and solution algorithms and served as the code's primary technical reference for users and researchers.
- **Engelman, M.S. (1993).** *CFD — An Industrial Perspective,* and contributions on *Incompressible Computational Fluid Dynamics* (1993), reflecting his transition from an academic to an industrial/commercial vantage point on CFD methodology.

FIDAP became one of the earliest and most widely adopted commercial general-purpose finite-element CFD packages, used across mechanical, chemical, materials, and process engineering applications — including viscoelastic and non-Newtonian flows, free-surface and moving-boundary problems, mold-filling and casting/solidification processes, continuous-casting flux-layer modeling, radiative heat transfer in participating media, and near-wall turbulence modeling using specialized finite elements coupled with the standard k–ε model. Trade press of the period (e.g., *Mechanical Engineering–CIME*, 1991) listed FIDAP from Fluid Dynamics International alongside FLUENT (Creare.x), PHOENICS (CHAM), FLOWTRAN (Compuflo), and FLOW3D (Flow Science) as one of the handful of major commercial CFD codes driving the field in the early 1990s.

Under Engelman's technical leadership, FDI and FIDAP became recognized fixtures of the CFD conference circuit, with Engelman himself an active contributor and organizer within the numerical-methods-in-fluids community — for example, editing/contributing to *Advances in Finite Element Analysis in Fluid Dynamics*, Proceedings of the ASME Winter Annual Meeting Symposium, Atlanta, GA (1991), and publishing benchmark and validation studies such as *Transient flow past a circular cylinder: A benchmark solution* (International Journal for Numerical Methods in Fluids, 1990), addressing the classic von Kármán vortex-street validation problem for unsteady Navier–Stokes solvers.

## Acquisition by Fluent and Later Corporate History

Fluid Dynamics International operated as an independent CFD software vendor through the 1980s and into the mid-1990s. On **16 May 1996**, FDI was acquired by **Fluent, Inc.** (at the time recently formed from the merger of Creare.x's CFD business with Aavid Thermalloy and newly public via IPO), which sought to broaden its simulation technology portfolio. The acquisition brought FIDAP — and its underlying finite-element technology, distinct from FLUENT's own finite-volume solver — into the Fluent product family, alongside the contemporaneous acquisition of Polyflow S.A. (viscoelastic flow software). FIDAP continued to be marketed and developed as a Fluent, Inc. product into the 2000s.

Fluent, Inc. was itself acquired by **ANSYS, Inc.** in a transaction announced in February 2006 and valued at approximately $565 million, folding the FIDAP lineage into the ANSYS simulation software portfolio, where elements of its finite-element incompressible-flow technology and validation heritage persist within ANSYS's broader CFD offerings.

## Scientific Legacy

Engelman's dual identity as both a research mathematician and a commercial software architect is central to his significance in the history of computational science:

1. **Foundational numerical analysis contributions.** His work with Gresho, Sani, Lee, and Griffiths on spurious pressure modes, penalty methods, and element stability for incompressible Navier–Stokes finite elements addressed core well-posedness and discretization issues that remain part of the standard graduate curriculum in computational fluid dynamics and finite-element theory.
2. **Bridging theory and industrial practice.** Through FIDAP, Engelman translated finite-element research — much of it his own — directly into a production tool used across industry and academia, at a time when finite-volume, structured-grid methods (PHOENICS, FLUENT, FLOW3D) dominated the nascent commercial CFD landscape. FIDAP represented one of the principal commercial vehicles demonstrating that unstructured finite-element methods could be engineered into robust, general-purpose industrial simulation software.
3. **Institutional legacy.** As co-founder of FDI, Engelman helped establish one of a small number of companies — alongside CHAM, Creare.x/Fluent, Computational Dynamics, Flow Science, AEA/CFX, and CFDRC — that collectively created the commercial CFD software industry in the 1980s. Through its acquisition by Fluent and subsequent absorption into ANSYS, FDI's technology and validation legacy remain embedded, in a mediated form, within one of today's dominant engineering simulation software platforms.
