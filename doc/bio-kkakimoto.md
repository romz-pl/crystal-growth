# Koichi Kakimoto: An Exhaustive Scientific Biography

## Overview

Koichi Kakimoto is a Japanese materials scientist and one of the world's foremost authorities on crystal growth engineering, particularly the numerical modeling and process control of bulk semiconductor and oxide single-crystal growth. Over a research career spanning more than four decades — from industrial research at NEC through professorships at Kyushu University and, most recently, Tohoku University — he has built a body of work bridging fundamental transport physics (melt convection, heat and mass transfer, dislocation dynamics) with the industrial-scale production of silicon, gallium arsenide, silicon carbide, gallium nitride, and gallium oxide (β-Ga₂O₃) crystals. He has served as President of the International Organization for Crystal Growth (IOCG) and is a co-recipient of the Frank Prize, one of the field's highest honors.

## Research Focus and Scientific Contributions

Kakimoto's research programme centers on **the physics and numerical simulation of bulk single-crystal growth**, with an emphasis on how melt convection, heat/mass transport, and externally applied fields (magnetic fields, in particular) govern crystal quality, dopant/impurity distribution, and defect (dislocation) formation. Major threads include:

### 1. Melt convection and transport phenomena in Czochralski (CZ) silicon growth
Some of his earliest and most cited work addressed **oxygen transport and segregation in CZ silicon** under the influence of vertical magnetic fields, combining direct experimental observation (including X-ray radiography of molten silicon) with large-scale numerical simulation of melt flow. This work helped establish quantitative links between applied magnetic field configuration, melt convection patterns, and dopant/impurity incorporation — knowledge directly relevant to industrial-scale silicon wafer production for microelectronics.

### 2. Dislocation dynamics and the Alexander–Haasen model
A sustained strand of his group's work has been the numerical modeling of **dislocation generation, multiplication, and propagation** during crystal growth and cooling, using and extending the Alexander–Haasen constitutive framework. Contributions include three-dimensional dislocation analyses linking cooling flux direction and crystal orientation to dislocation activation, and efficient/stable numerical implementations of the Alexander–Haasen model for practical process simulation — work with direct application to multicrystalline and mono-like silicon ingots for photovoltaics.

### 3. Silicon for photovoltaics (solar-grade silicon)
Kakimoto has been a major contributor to the science of **directionally solidified and seed-cast multicrystalline/mono-like silicon** for solar cells, addressing impurity distribution (e.g., SiN precipitates), residual strain, thermal donor formation, and the relationship between dislocation density and solar cell efficiency. This includes collaborative work on large seed-cast ingots (up to 50 cm scale) and their characterization.

### 4. Compound and wide-bandgap semiconductor crystal growth
His work extends beyond silicon to **GaAs, SiC (via physical vapor transport), GaN (including MOVPE process simulation), and III-nitride epitaxy** (e.g., In incorporation efficiency and structural stability in InGaN/InN systems), generally through a combination of thermal/fluid-dynamic simulation and process control analysis.

### 5. Gallium oxide (β-Ga₂O₃) crystal growth
In his more recent Tohoku University period, Kakimoto has been closely involved in advancing **melt-growth methods for β-Ga₂O₃**, an emerging ultra-wide-bandgap semiconductor of major interest for power electronics. This includes crucible-free/cold-crucible growth techniques designed to avoid expensive noble-metal crucibles, scale-up of crystal diameter and length, control of crystal twisting during Czochralski growth, and analysis of the influence of crystal/melt transparency (radiative heat transfer) on growth stability — connecting back to his long-standing expertise in radiative and convective heat transfer in melts.

### Methodological signature
Across these material systems, Kakimoto's characteristic approach is the **coupling of numerical/computational modeling (heat transfer, melt convection, dislocation mechanics, defect thermodynamics) with experimental crystal growth and characterization**, aimed at translating fundamental transport physics into practical process control for industrially relevant crystal growth furnaces (Czochralski, directional solidification, physical vapor transport, floating zone).

## Summary Assessment

Koichi Kakimoto's scientific career illustrates a distinctively Japanese model of materials-science leadership: sustained, decades-long engagement with a single overarching technical problem — how transport phenomena (heat, mass, momentum) inside a growth furnace determine the microstructural quality of a bulk crystal — pursued across a widening set of technologically critical materials (Si → GaAs → SiC/GaN → β-Ga₂O₃) as the semiconductor industry's frontier has shifted from microelectronics toward power electronics and wide-bandgap devices. His combination of deep numerical-modeling expertise with hands-on process engineering, his long institutional service to the international crystal growth community (culminating in the IOCG presidency and the 2025 Frank Prize), and his current position bridging Tohoku University's IMR and industry-facing innovation centers together mark him as one of the senior figures shaping both the fundamental science and the industrial practice of bulk single-crystal growth today.
