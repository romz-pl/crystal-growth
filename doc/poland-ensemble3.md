# ENSEMBLE3 and the Prospects for a Polish Compound-Semiconductor Crystal Growth and Wafer Manufacturing Ecosystem

*A technical and economic assessment*

---

## 1. Executive Summary

ENSEMBLE3 ("Centre of Excellence for nanophotonics, advanced materials and novel crystal growth-based technologies," also styled *e3*) is a Warsaw-based International Research Agenda / Teaming for Excellence Centre of Excellence, jointly established by the Łukasiewicz Research Network's Institute of Electronic Materials Technology (ŁUKASIEWICZ–ITME) and the Faculty of Chemistry of the University of Warsaw, with international partners including the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT), Sapienza University of Rome, and the CIC nanoGUNE Nanoscience Cooperative Research Centre (Spain). It is funded jointly through the EU Horizon 2020 "Teaming for Excellence" instrument (GA No. 857543) and the Foundation for Polish Science's International Research Agendas (MAB) programme (GA No. MAB/2020/14), with combined committed funding on the order of €15–30 million depending on phase and reporting source, running from September 2019 through September 2026.

The Centre's technical core is directly relevant to compound semiconductor wafer manufacturing: it operates a working crystal-growth pilot-production line for III–V binary compound semiconductors — GaAs, InAs, GaP, InP, GaSb, and InSb — alongside an oxide single-crystal group (YAG, YAP, GdCOB, NGO, SBN) serving photonic and laser applications. ENSEMBLE3 markets itself as one of very few producers of GaP substrates in Europe and positions itself as a candidate global leader in that specific niche, since GaP is not manufactured at meaningful volume by any EU or North American competitor.

This report evaluates ENSEMBLE3 as a prospective R&D and industrial partner for building out a Polish compound-semiconductor crystal-growth and wafer ecosystem. The central finding is that ENSEMBLE3 is a credible, internationally networked, well-instrumented **research and pilot-production** partner with genuine institutional lineage in Czochralski-based crystal growth (tracing to Jan Czochralski's own return to Warsaw in the 1920s), but that it is **not, in its current form, an industrial-scale wafer fab or foundry-adjacent supplier**. Its value proposition to Poland is as the technological anchor and talent/IP source for a future ecosystem — analogous to the role IISB/Fraunhofer or Leibniz IKZ play in Germany — rather than as a drop-in substitute for a merchant compound-semiconductor manufacturer such as IQE, Freiberger Compound Materials, or AXT. Converting ENSEMBLE3's capability into an industrial-scale ecosystem will require capital-intensive scale-up (larger boule diameters, higher throughput growth systems, dedicated wafering/polishing lines, and quality systems compliant with automotive/RF/photonics customer qualification), most plausibly financed through the EU Chips Act's Pillar II state-aid mechanisms, the Chips Joint Undertaking, and/or private strategic investment, with ENSEMBLE3 (or a spin-out) as technology provider and Łukasiewicz–ITME/University of Warsaw as the institutional continuity layer.

### 2.3 Technical infrastructure and capability

ENSEMBLE3's stated activity spans the full "TRL ladder" segment from basic research through pilot production:

- **Basic research** — identification of candidate material systems; new crystal-growth process development; nanoscale optical phenomena in grown materials.
- **Applied research** — process optimisation for specific device-relevant properties (defect density, doping uniformity, optical/electronic quality).
- **Pilot production** — small-batch manufacturing of both oxide single crystals and III–V compound semiconductor boules/wafers for external customers and internal device development.

Reported crystal systems:

- **Oxide single crystals**: YAG (Y₃Al₅O₁₂), YAP (YAlO₃), GdCOB (Ca₄GdO(BO₃)₃), NGO (NdGaO₃), SBN (Sr₀.₆₁Ba₀.₃₉Nb₂O₆) — used in laser gain media, scintillators, and nonlinear/electro-optic devices.
- **III–V binary compound semiconductors**: GaAs, InAs, GaP, InP, GaSb, InSb — used across LEDs, laser diodes, photodetectors, IR sensing, and RF/power electronics depending on compound.

The GaP position is the most commercially distinctive claim: ENSEMBLE3 asserts that GaP substrate manufacturing is essentially absent from both EU and North American ("NAS") industrial capacity — a claim consistent with the historical CORDIS record of an earlier EU-funded GaP substrate project (IST-2001-32793) that explicitly framed GaP domestic substrate manufacturing as a strategic EU gap for LED supply chains, a gap that appears to have persisted for roughly two decades. If accurate and current, this would make ENSEMBLE3 a rare Western alternative to Asian GaP suppliers, though the report authors note this claim originates from ENSEMBLE3's own marketing material (trade-fair exhibitor copy) and should be independently verified against current global GaP capacity (principal producers historically include Sumitomo Electric and Freiberger, alongside Chinese suppliers) before being treated as a settled competitive fact.

The growth method underpinning ENSEMBLE3's silicon-adjacent heritage is the **Czochralski (CZ) technique** and its melt-growth relatives; the Centre explicitly frames its identity around Jan Czochralski's 1916 discovery and his subsequent return to interwar Warsaw Polytechnic. For III–V compounds specifically, industrial practice typically uses **Liquid-Encapsulated Czochralski (LEC)**, **Vertical Gradient Freeze (VGF)**, or **Vertical Bridgman (VB)** variants rather than open CZ, owing to the high vapour pressure of the group-V element (As, P, Sb) at melting temperature; publicly available ENSEMBLE3 material does not specify which variant is used for each compound, which is a material technical detail a due-diligence process should confirm directly with the Centre.

## 3. Technical Context: Why Compound-Semiconductor Bulk Growth Is Hard and Strategically Valuable

### 3.1 The economics and physics of III–V bulk growth versus silicon

Silicon CZ growth benefits from a mature, standardised, high-volume global supply chain: pullers up to 300+ mm diameter, well-characterised thermal/fluid-dynamic models, and impurity control refined over six decades. III–V compound bulk growth is intrinsically harder and lower-volume for several coupled physical reasons:

1. **Congruent vaporisation / stoichiometry control.** Compounds such as GaAs, InP, and GaSb have a group-V element with substantial equilibrium vapour pressure at the melting point (e.g. arsenic over GaAs melt), which without encapsulation (boric oxide, B₂O₃, in LEC) or a sealed ampoule (VGF/VB) leads to melt decomposition, stoichiometric drift, and precipitate/defect formation. Managing this constrains reactor design, crucible chemistry, and achievable growth rate.
2. **Thermal-stress-driven dislocation generation.** III–V compounds generally have lower critical resolved shear stress and higher thermal-stress sensitivity than silicon, so radial and axial temperature gradients during growth and cool-down readily nucleate dislocations. This links directly to the melt-convection and interface-shape physics (Marangoni convection at a free melt surface, forced convection from crystal/crucible rotation, and the resulting non-axisymmetric or oscillatory flow transitions) that dominate boule quality — exactly the continuum melt-growth phenomena (segregation at the growth interface, radiative heat transfer in the hot zone, dimensionless-group scaling of the relevant instabilities) that process-simulation tools such as CGSim, CrysMAS, FEMAG-CZ, and CrysVUn are built to model.
3. **Segregation and doping uniformity.** The effective segregation coefficient for dopants and unintentional impurities in III–V melts is frequently far from unity, producing axial and radial resistivity gradients that must be actively managed (rotation rate, growth-rate profiling, magnetic damping) — a materially harder control problem than in silicon CZ.
4. **Scale economics.** III–V wafer markets are fragmented by compound and application (GaAs for RF/mm-wave and LED/VCSEL, InP for photonics/optical communications, GaSb/InSb for infrared detection, GaP for visible LEDs and as a lattice-matched buffer), so no single compound supports silicon-scale volume; this caps achievable economies of scale and keeps per-wafer cost structurally higher, reinforcing niche/specialty rather than commodity market dynamics.

### 3.2 Strategic relevance

Despite the smaller absolute market size relative to silicon, compound semiconductors sit on several EU-designated strategic technology pathways: RF/mm-wave devices for 5G/6G and defence (GaAs, GaN-on-substrate ecosystems), photonic integrated circuits and optical interconnect for AI-data-centre and telecom infrastructure (InP), infrared sensing for automotive, defence, and space (InSb, GaSb), and photonics-enabled quantum and sensing technologies more broadly (oxide crystals for laser gain/nonlinear optics). The European Chips Act's stated ambition to secure EU semiconductor "supply chain autonomy" (Regulation (EU) 2023/1781) and the subsequent 2026 "Chips Act 2.0" proposal explicitly identify diversification beyond leading-edge digital logic — including substrate and materials capacity — as part of resilience policy, which is the funding context in which an ENSEMBLE3-anchored Polish ecosystem would most plausibly be financed.

## 4. ENSEMBLE3 as a Prospective Partner: Capability-by-Capability Assessment

### 4.1 Strengths

- **Genuine institutional and technical lineage.** ŁUKASIEWICZ–ITME (formerly ITME, Instytut Technologii Materiałów Elektronicznych) has decades of prior Polish state-institute experience in electronic-materials crystal growth predating ENSEMBLE3's creation, giving the Centre continuity of tacit process knowledge rather than a cold start.
- **Working pilot-production line across multiple III–V compounds.** The breadth (GaAs, InAs, GaP, InP, GaSb, InSb) is unusual for a single European centre and suggests genuine multi-compound growth infrastructure (likely multiple furnace/puller configurations and encapsulation/ampoule-sealing capability) rather than a single-material demonstrator.
- **International partner network with complementary expertise.** KIT (materials/nanotechnology), Sapienza (photonics/materials physics), and nanoGUNE (nanoscience, plasmonics) extend ENSEMBLE3's reach into device-integration and characterisation science beyond what a standalone crystal-growth institute would offer, and create pre-existing EU collaboration channels useful for Chips JU or Horizon Europe consortium-building.
- **A differentiated market niche (GaP).** If ENSEMBLE3's claim of near-unique EU GaP substrate capability holds up under independent verification, this is a genuine strategic asset: it offers a low-competition entry point for a Polish ecosystem to establish an initial export/qualification foothold before attempting to compete in more contested compounds (GaAs, InP) against incumbents such as Freiberger Compound Materials, AXT, Sumitomo Electric, or IQE.
- **Funding runway and EU policy alignment.** The Centre sits squarely inside two funding logics — Teaming for Excellence (capacity-building) and, going forward, potential Chips Act / Chips JU pillar-II and pillar-III instruments (state aid, "first-of-a-kind" facilities, Semiconductor Coalition initiatives that Poland has already joined alongside Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the Netherlands) — that materially improve its ability to attract follow-on capital.
- **Location and talent pipeline.** Proximity to the University of Warsaw and Warsaw University of Technology (Czochralski's own institution) provides an academic talent pipeline for crystal growth, materials characterisation, and device physics.

### 4.2 Limitations and open questions for due diligence

- **Scale gap to industrial wafer supply.** "Pilot production" and "small-scale production of high-purity" compounds (the Centre's own characterisation) is explicitly sub-industrial. No public information available to this report specifies boule diameters, annual throughput, wafer yield, or defect-density (etch-pit density / dislocation density) specifications achieved — all of which are the actual gating parameters for whether ENSEMBLE3 material could qualify with a downstream epitaxy or device fabricator today.
- **Photonics/materials-science orientation versus semiconductor-device/foundry orientation.** The Scientific Advisory Board and stated research themes (metamaterials, plasmonics, eutectic self-organisation, nanophotonics) indicate a centre of gravity in advanced materials physics rather than high-volume manufacturing engineering, process-control automation, or semiconductor-fab quality systems (SPC, IATF/ISO 9100-class quality management, customer PPAP processes) that industrial compound-semiconductor customers require.
- **Unclear vertical integration and epitaxy linkage.** Bulk substrate manufacturing is one link in the value chain; industrial competitiveness typically requires either in-house epitaxial capability (as at IQE) or firmly established qualified relationships with epi-foundries and device makers. Public material does not clarify ENSEMBLE3's position on this axis.
- **Capital intensity of scale-up.** Moving from pilot to industrial throughput for III–V bulk growth requires substantial capital: additional pullers/furnaces, ampoule-sealing and pBN crucible supply chains, wafering (ID/OD or wire saws), lapping/polishing lines, metrology (XRD, PL mapping, resistivity mapping, EPD/dislocation counting), and cleanroom-adjacent handling — likely tens to low hundreds of millions of euros depending on target volume, a scale of investment ENSEMBLE3 as a research centre is not structured to self-finance.
- **Project sunset risk.** The H2020 Teaming project itself concludes September 2026; the legal entity ENSEMBLE3 sp. z o.o. is intended to persist beyond that date, but the transition from grant-funded Centre of Excellence to a self-sustaining or industrially-integrated entity is a normal risk point for Teaming-for-Excellence-type centres across the EU (a pattern also seen in other Teaming/Widening projects), and prospective partners should specifically diligence ENSEMBLE3's post-2026 institutional and financial plan.
- **Verification of competitive claims.** As noted in §2.3, the "no significant EU/NAS GaP competitor" claim and "world's leading manufacturer" framing appear in ENSEMBLE3's own promotional material; an independent market-intelligence check (against Freiberger, Sumitomo Electric, AXT, Yamanaka, and Chinese GaP suppliers such as Tongmei/China Crystal Technologies) is warranted before this is used as an investment thesis input.

## 5. Building a Polish Compound-Semiconductor Ecosystem Around ENSEMBLE3: A Structural View

### 5.1 What "ecosystem" requires beyond a crystal-growth centre

A functioning national compound-semiconductor ecosystem typically needs, at minimum:

1. **Feedstock and precursor supply** (high-purity Ga, As, In, P, Sb source materials, pBN/quartz crucibles, boric-oxide encapsulant).
2. **Bulk crystal growth** (ENSEMBLE3's current core competence).
3. **Wafering and surface finishing** (slicing, lapping, polishing, cleaning to device-ready specification).
4. **Epitaxy** (MOCVD/MBE) — typically a separate, capital-intensive step performed either in-house or by dedicated epi-foundries.
5. **Device fabrication / packaging** — the eventual customer base (RF, photonics, LED, sensor manufacturers).
6. **Metrology, qualification, and standards infrastructure** enabling customer sign-off (PPAP-equivalent processes for compound-semiconductor markets).
7. **Workforce pipeline** (crystal-growth engineers, process technicians, quality engineers) — an area where the University of Warsaw/Warsaw University of Technology linkage is a genuine asset.
8. **Capital formation mechanism** — a blend of EU structural/Chips Act funds, Polish national co-financing, and private/strategic investment.

ENSEMBLE3 today plausibly covers items 2 and, to a limited degree, 3 and 6, at pilot scale, and contributes meaningfully to item 7 through its university linkages. It does not appear, from public material, to cover items 1, 4, or 5, meaning a genuine ecosystem play requires either ENSEMBLE3 expanding its remit (unlikely given its research-centre mandate and funding structure) or — more plausibly — ENSEMBLE3 acting as the technological anchor within a broader consortium that brings in industrial partners for epitaxy, device fabrication, and precursor supply.

### 5.2 Comparable European models

- **Freiberger Compound Materials (Germany)** — a dedicated industrial GaAs/InP substrate manufacturer, illustrating the scale and specialisation an industrial-grade successor to ENSEMBLE3's pilot line would need to reach.
- **Leibniz-Institut für Kristallzüchtung (IKZ, Berlin)** and **Fraunhofer IISB (Erlangen)** — publicly funded research institutes with deep crystal-growth science (melt convection, interface modelling, simulation-tool development) that maintain close industrial partnerships without themselves being volume manufacturers; this is closer to ENSEMBLE3's plausible steady-state role than a Freiberger-style manufacturing identity.
- **IQE plc (UK/US)** — a vertically integrated compound-semiconductor substrate-to-epiwafer supplier, illustrating the vertical-integration path a future Polish champion might eventually pursue if it can secure the capital and customer base.

### 5.3 Funding and policy levers available to Poland

- **Poland's existing Semiconductor Coalition membership** (alongside Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the Netherlands, as of March 2025) signals political commitment to semiconductor strategic autonomy at the EU level and creates a plausible channel for Chips Act Pillar II/III co-financing.
- **The proposed European Chips Act 2.0** (Commission proposal adopted June 2026) is explicitly reorienting toward demand-side creation and diversification beyond leading-edge logic, and away from a pure supply-side subsidy race — a reorientation that, if it materialises as drafted, would favour a specialty/niche play (such as GaP or other compound substrates) over an attempt to compete head-on in commodity silicon or advanced-node logic, both of which would be poor fits for Poland's current industrial base.
- **Existing Polish industrial base in adjacent photonics/microelectronics** — the Polish Technology Platform for Photonics (PPTF) reports roughly 250 companies in Poland's photonics and microelectronics sector, and industry actors (e.g. VIGO Photonics, itself a significant Polish IR-detector and compound-semiconductor-adjacent manufacturer) have publicly called for a national supply-chain mapping exercise and a "Polish Microelectronics and Photonics Platform" — indicating both a plausible customer base for ENSEMBLE3-grown substrates and an existing industry voice that could co-anchor a national ecosystem strategy alongside ENSEMBLE3.
- **Intel's Poland investment** (a ~$4.6 billion assembly-and-test facility, roughly one-third Polish-government co-financed) demonstrates Poland's capacity to structure large co-financed semiconductor deals, though that facility is back-end assembly/test rather than front-end fabrication or materials, and is not directly synergistic with a compound-semiconductor substrate strategy beyond demonstrating state capacity for large deal structuring.

### 5.4 Risks specific to a Poland-centred strategy

- **Talent and equipment-supplier concentration.** Crystal-growth furnace/puller equipment and high-purity precursor supply chains are globally concentrated (Japan, Germany, US, China); Poland would be a price-taker on capital equipment and potentially exposed to export-control regimes on dual-use crystal-growth and semiconductor-materials technology.
- **Customer qualification cycles.** Compound-semiconductor substrate qualification with downstream device makers typically takes multiple years per material/application combination; a Polish ecosystem's revenue ramp would likely lag capital deployment by several years even under a well-executed plan.
- **Competition for EU Chips Act funds.** Germany, France, and other larger semiconductor ecosystems are also competing for Chips Act 2.0 resources; Poland/ENSEMBLE3's realistic positioning is a specialty-niche (e.g., GaP, or oxide crystals for photonics) rather than a broad-front compound-semiconductor strategy.

## 6. Synthesis and Recommendations

1. **Treat ENSEMBLE3 as the natural technology and talent anchor, not the industrial manufacturer, of a Polish compound-semiconductor strategy.** Its comparative advantage is deep crystal-growth science, multi-compound pilot capability, and an EU-network institutional platform — the same role Fraunhofer IISB or Leibniz IKZ play in Germany relative to Freiberger's manufacturing scale.
2. **Prioritise the GaP niche for initial industrial scale-up**, subject to independent verification of ENSEMBLE3's current defect-density, diameter, and throughput specifications against realistic customer requirements (LED, and potentially GaP-based tandem/optoelectronic applications), given the apparently thin EU/NAS competitive field in this specific compound.
3. **Structure any industrial partnership as a consortium**, bringing in (a) an epitaxy partner (in-house build-out or an external MOCVD/MBE foundry relationship), (b) a Polish or EU device-fabrication customer base (VIGO Photonics and the broader ~250-company Polish photonics/microelectronics cluster are a plausible initial demand anchor), and (c) precursor/feedstock supply arrangements, rather than expecting ENSEMBLE3 alone to vertically integrate.
4. **Pursue EU Chips Act Pillar II/III and Chips JU financing explicitly through Poland's existing Semiconductor Coalition membership**, framing the proposal around supply-chain diversification and strategic-autonomy criteria that the Chips Act 2.0 process is emphasising, rather than competing for leading-edge logic funding where Poland has no realistic near-term positioning.
5. **Commission focused technical due diligence** before any capital commitment, specifically covering: (a) achieved boule diameter and annual throughput by compound; (b) defect density (EPD/dislocation density) and resistivity uniformity data against customer specifications; (c) which growth variant (LEC/VGF/VB) is used per compound and its scalability; (d) ENSEMBLE3's post-2026 (post-Teaming-grant) institutional and financial continuity plan; and (e) an independent market-share verification of the GaP competitive-landscape claim.
6. **Leverage the University of Warsaw / Warsaw University of Technology talent pipeline explicitly** in any ecosystem plan, given the acute global shortage of crystal-growth process engineers and the multi-year lead time to train them — this is arguably as durable an ENSEMBLE3 asset as its physical growth infrastructure.

## 8. Caveats on Sourcing

Much of the descriptive material on ENSEMBLE3's specific technical capabilities (growth methods per compound, defect densities, achieved diameters, current production volumes) is drawn from the Centre's own promotional and institutional-reporting material rather than independently audited technical disclosures or peer-reviewed process papers describing the pilot line itself. Figures for combined funding (€15M vs. €30M across sources) are inconsistent between the H2020/KIT reporting and Polish institutional reporting, likely reflecting different accounting scopes (EU Teaming grant alone vs. Teaming + IRA co-financing combined) rather than a factual error, but this should be reconciled directly with ENSEMBLE3 or the Foundation for Polish Science before use in any investment memorandum. Readers using this report for capital-allocation decisions should treat Sections 2.3, 4.1 (GaP claim), and 5.3 (funding-figure claims) as requiring primary-source verification.
