# Dietrich Schwabe — Scientific Biography

**Professor Emeritus, I. Physikalisches Institut, Justus Liebig University Giessen (JLU Giessen)**

## Overview

Dietrich Schwabe is a German experimental physicist, now Professor Emeritus at the I. Physikalisches Institut of Justus Liebig University Giessen, whose career centers on interfacial fluid dynamics — above all thermocapillary (Marangoni) convection — and its consequences for melt crystal growth. Over a publication record spanning more than four decades (earliest traceable work from the early 1980s through papers as late as 2020), Schwabe built one of the most sustained experimental programs worldwide on surface-tension-driven flow instabilities, using both laboratory model systems and a long series of microgravity experiments flown on sounding rockets (TEXUS, MAXUS), parabolic aircraft, Spacelab, and the Mir space station. His work sits at the intersection of fundamental fluid instability physics and applied crystal-growth engineering, and it is frequently cited in both the fluid-dynamics and crystal-growth literatures.

## Research Program and Scientific Contributions

### 1. Thermocapillary (Marangoni) Convection — Core Research Theme

The unifying thread of Schwabe's career is the experimental study of **Marangoni convection**: flow driven by surface-tension gradients along a free liquid interface, arising from temperature or concentration gradients. He investigated this phenomenon across a wide range of model geometries:

- **Floating zones / liquid bridges** (cylindrical liquid columns held by surface tension between two solid disks, modeling the float-zone crystal-growth technique), where he characterized the transition from steady, axisymmetric thermocapillary flow to time-dependent, oscillatory, and eventually chaotic states as a function of the Marangoni number.
- **Open cavities and side-heated liquid layers**, including annular gaps, rectangular cavities, and shallow layers heated from the side or from below (Bénard–Marangoni instability).
- **Czochralski-configuration model systems**, in which buoyant, thermocapillary, and forced (crystal-rotation-driven) convection interact in an open crucible.

Across these geometries, he identified and characterized a series of named instability phenomena, including:

- **Hydrothermal waves (HTWs)** — traveling thermal/velocity waves that develop on thermocapillary-driven free surfaces above a critical Marangoni number, studied by Schwabe in liquid bridges, annuli, and floating zones under both terrestrial and microgravity conditions.
- **Standing waves (SWs) in thermocapillary layers**, revisited and reconciled with hydrothermal-wave theory in later work (with C. Bach, 2015).
- **The multi-roll structure (MRS)** of thermocapillary flow in long floating zones and thin side-heated liquid layers, including its transition to oscillatory flow and "restationarization" above the critical Marangoni number.
- **Three-dimensional stationary instabilities** (counter-rotating longitudinal roll pairs) in dynamic thermocapillary shallow cavities (with S. Benz).
- **Surface waves excited by resonant coalescence** of non-coalescing hot and cold liquid volumes — a distinctive nonlinear excitation mechanism for standing gravity–capillary waves (with P. Hintz).
- **Convective instabilities under combined horizontal and vertical (inclined) temperature gradients** in liquid layers with a free upper surface (with A. Mizev).
- **Instability of Marangoni flow in the presence of insoluble surfactants**, connecting classical thermocapillary instability theory to surfactant-laden interfaces relevant to biological and industrial films (with A. Mizev, A. Shmyrova, A. Viviani).

### 2. Particle Accumulation Structures (PAS)

Schwabe is credited as the discoverer/originator of **Particle Accumulation Structures (PAS)** — a striking phenomenon in which small tracer particles suspended in an oscillatory (time-dependent) thermocapillary flow spontaneously self-organize into coherent, quasi-one-dimensional strings or "necklaces" that co-rotate with the underlying hydrothermal wave, rather than being passively dispersed by the flow. First reported in floating-zone experiments in the 1990s, PAS became a signature diagnostic for the presence and structure of hydrothermal waves and was studied by Schwabe and collaborators (P. Hintz, S. Frank, A. Mizev, S. Tanaka, H. Kawamura, I. Ueno) across a series of papers from the mid-1990s through 2011, including studies of:

- Formation dynamics and existence ranges of PAS as a function of Marangoni number and liquid-bridge aspect ratio.
- Behavior of particles of different density ratios (denser and lighter than the host fluid) in both traveling and standing hydrothermal waves.
- PAS as a possible **model for a step in planetary formation** — the concentration of solid particles within toroidal vortical flows in protoplanetary-disk-like settings, a conceptual link Schwabe drew explicitly in a 1999 paper with S. Frank.

### 3. Microgravity Experimentation

A defining feature of Schwabe's career is direct experimentation under reduced gravity, needed to isolate pure thermocapillary (surface-tension-driven) flow from buoyancy-driven convection, which dominates equivalent systems on Earth. His microgravity campaigns, spanning roughly 1981 to 2014, included:

- **Sounding rocket flights**: TEXUS (TEXUS IIIa, IIIb, 5, 8, 21) and MAXUS (MAXUS 1, MAXUS 2, MAXUS 4) missions, used to measure critical Marangoni numbers for the laminar–oscillatory transition in floating zones and liquid bridges, and to study hydrothermal waves in liquid bridges near the Rayleigh (stability) limit of aspect ratio.
- **Parabolic aircraft flights** (KC-135), used for shorter-duration microgravity tests of flat liquid–gas interfaces and their stability (with J.C. Legros and others).
- **Spacelab missions**, notably the German D1 mission (1985), which carried the **MKB (Marangoni-Konvektion im offenen Boot / "Marangoni Convection in an Open Boat")** experiment, comparing microgravity and ground-based thermocapillary flow in a rectangular open cavity.
- **The Mir space station**, where the **MAUS G141** experiment (flown during STS-89's docking phase, January 1998) established NaNO₃-melt floating zones under microgravity for ~14 hours to study hydrothermal waves and standing-wave behavior at controlled axial temperature gradients.
- **The MAGIA experiment** (flown on the Russian FOTON-12 satellite), investigating thermocapillary flow structures in a side-heated annular gap with a free upper surface.
- Later rocket-based work (2014–2015) revisited critical Marangoni numbers for liquid bridges under microgravity and synthesized "results and lessons learned" from this multi-decade microgravity program in a retrospective review.

This microgravity work situates Schwabe within the German and European space-materials-science community, with documented collaborations connecting to DLR (Deutsches Zentrum für Luft- und Raumfahrt) and European Space Agency (ESA)-affiliated researchers, and with recurring co-authors including S. Benz, R. Velten, F. Preisser, and J. Metzger.

### 4. Melt Crystal Growth: Czochralski, Floating Zone, and Related Techniques

Building on his fluid-instability work, Schwabe made sustained contributions to the physics of industrial and laboratory melt-growth techniques, particularly the **Czochralski method**:

- **Convection in the Czochralski crucible**, both for non-rotating and rotating crystal configurations, including the interaction of buoyant, thermocapillary, and forced convection driven by crystal rotation (two-part study with P. Hintz and H. Wilke, 2001).
- **Intermittent transitions to chaotic flow** driven by crystal rotation during Czochralski growth, identified and analyzed as a type-III intermittency phenomenon (with R.R. Sumathi, 2005).
- **Interface deflection and interface inversion** phenomena in Czochralski growth of high-melting-point oxides such as **YAG (Y₃Al₅O₁₂)**, including experimental and finite-element numerical modeling of the convex-to-concave transition of the crystal–melt interface as crystal radius increases in the shoulder region (with R.R. Sumathi, H. Wilke, 2004).
- **Internal (radiative) heat transfer** during Czochralski growth of YAG, including the design and operation of a custom high-temperature transmission spectrometer (visible to ~3.5 µm, up to 1400 °C) to measure crystal transmission spectra as direct input for radiative heat-transport calculations (two-part series with A. Polity, Z. Gałązka, H. Wilke, L. Ackermann, K. Dupré, 2003).
- **Spiral morphology / spiral growth** of Czochralski-grown crystals, particularly high-melting-point oxides — an unwanted growth defect that reduces usable crystal yield. Schwabe produced a sustained analysis of this phenomenon across three papers (2011, 2016, 2019), developing a physical model distinguishing the boundary conditions that permit spiral growth from the triggering events that initiate it, including the identification of a "foot" structure developing in the meniscus region at the onset of spiral growth (with R. Uecker, M. Bernhagen, Z. Gałązka).
- **Flow-suspended solution growth**, a novel crystal-growth method in which the growing crystal is suspended without wall contact in an up-flowing, supersaturated solution stream within a conical growth tube (with A. Mizev, 2009).
- **Temperature-gradient double-crucible method** for high-temperature solution (flux) growth of compounds with limited solubility or narrow stability ranges (with P.A. Jung, A. Scharmann, 1995).
- Model experiments and computational studies of **instabilities in Czochralski-type flow** for large-Prandtl-number oxide melts, including identification of cold-plume detachment and baroclinic instability mechanisms (with M. Teitel, A. Gelfgat, 2008).
- Earlier crystal-growth work included **single-crystal growth of BiLiF₄** by the Czochralski method under fluorinating atmosphere, together with structural, vibrational (Raman), and optical spectroscopic characterization of BiLiF₄ and YLiF₄, and of Pr³⁺-doped BiLiF₄ (with E. Schultheiss and A. Scharmann, 1984–1987/2006).
- Contributions on **directional solidification**, including the interaction of thermocapillary and solutocapillary (concentration-driven) forces on flow instability and solidification speed for melts containing surface-active impurities (with X. Da, A. Scharmann, 1996).

### 5. Broader Interfacial Fluid Dynamics

Beyond crystal growth, Schwabe's interfacial fluid dynamics work extended to:

- Coupling and decoupling of **buoyant and thermocapillary convection** in open rectangular gaps, using holographic interferometry and tracer-particle flow visualization (with H. Dürr, J. Metzger).
- **Thermocapillary flow without return flow ("linear flow")**, an experimental realization of a flow regime proposed theoretically by Smith and Davis, achieved by engineering channel geometries with lower flow resistance than the return-flow path (with N.A. Ospennikov, 2004).
- Editorial and synthesis work: co-editor (with R. Narayanan) of a **Lecture Notes in Physics volume on Interfacial Fluid Dynamics and Transport Processes** (2003), collecting topical lectures and tutorial reviews on interfacial instabilities and driven flows relevant to materials science, melting/solidification, electrodeposition, and biomembranes.
- A book-chapter contribution on **axisymmetric fluid systems** and the formation of dynamical structures therein, alongside G. Pfister, F. Busse, and S.C. Müller.

### 6. A Late, Unrelated Foray: Honeybee Comb Mechanics

Notably, in 2020 — well into his emeritus period — Schwabe co-authored (with beekeeper/researcher Robert Oeder) a study applying physical reasoning outside his core field: an investigation of the **upward tilt of honeycomb cells in *Apis mellifera* combs** (published in *Apidologie*), experimentally testing (and refuting) the traditional beekeeping explanation that the ~13° cell tilt exists to prevent honey outflow. By hanging empty combs upside down in active hives, the study showed inverted combs retained honey normally, and proposed instead that the tilt increases the mechanical carrying capacity of the comb. This paper stands out as a rare application of Schwabe's physical/experimental instincts to a biomechanical rather than crystal-growth or fluid-instability problem.
