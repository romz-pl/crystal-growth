# Poland's National Framework for Supporting Strategic Semiconductor Investments and the Development of Semiconductor Bulk Crystal and Wafer Manufacturing

## Abstract

Poland's *National Framework for Supporting Strategic Semiconductor Investments* (Polish: *Krajowe Ramy Wspierania Strategicznych Inwestycji Półprzewodnikowych*, KRWSIP), adopted by Resolution of the Council of Ministers on 8 December 2023 pursuant to Regulation (EU) 2023/1781 (the "European Chips Act"), constitutes Poland's principal state-aid and coordination instrument for building domestic semiconductor manufacturing capacity. While public discourse on the framework has emphasized back-end activities — assembly, testing, and packaging, exemplified by Intel's Wrocław investment — the instrument is, by design, value-chain-agnostic and applicable to front-end and materials-stage activities, including the growth of bulk single crystals (Si, SiC, GaN, sapphire, and related compound semiconductors) and their conversion into polished wafers and epitaxial substrates. This report analyzes the structure of the framework, its legal basis in the EU Chips Act, and the specific mechanisms — funding instruments, "first-of-a-kind" facility status, regulatory fast-tracking, and ecosystem coordination — through which it can support the establishment, expansion, and long-term competitiveness of substrate manufacturers operating in Poland. It also assesses Poland's existing crystal-growth and materials science base (notably Ammono and Łukasiewicz–IMiF/ITME), identifies structural gaps relative to the requirements of substrate manufacturing (capital intensity, feedstock supply, energy costs, cleanroom infrastructure, and skilled crystal-growth engineering talent), and offers a framework-aligned roadmap for strengthening Poland's position in the upstream materials segment of the European semiconductor supply chain.

---

## 1. Introduction and Scope

### 1.1 Why bulk crystal and wafer manufacturing matters

Semiconductor substrates — wafers cut from bulk single crystals of silicon (Si), silicon carbide (SiC), gallium nitride (GaN), gallium arsenide (GaAs), and related compounds — are the foundational input to the entire downstream value chain: epitaxy, device fabrication, packaging, and assembly. The European Union imports the overwhelming majority of its substrate-grade material from outside Europe (Si wafers predominantly from Asia; SiC substrates concentrated among a small number of US, Japanese, and increasingly Chinese producers; GaN bulk crystals from a handful of specialist firms worldwide). This concentration is widely identified — by the European Commission, industry associations such as SEMI, and national strategy documents — as a structural vulnerability for European "chip sovereignty," since front-end wafer supply sits upstream of, and is a precondition for, the packaging and assembly capacity Europe has more successfully attracted to date.

### 1.2 Scope of this report

This report examines:
1. The legal, institutional, and financial architecture of Poland's National Framework and its parent instrument, the EU Chips Act;
2. How the framework's specific mechanisms map onto the economics and risk profile of bulk crystal growth and wafer fabrication as a business (as distinct from packaging/assembly or design);
3. The existing Polish substrate and materials ecosystem — Ammono (ammonothermal GaN), Łukasiewicz–IMiF (successor to ITME/ITE), and adjacent academic and pilot-line infrastructure;
4. Gaps and risks specific to substrate manufacturing that the framework, as currently constituted, does not fully address; and
5. Policy recommendations for using the framework, and its successor "Policy for the Semiconductor Sector 2025+," to deliberately cultivate a competitive Polish substrate segment.

## 2. Legal and Institutional Basis

### 2.1 The European Chips Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/1781)

The National Framework is a national implementing instrument for Regulation (EU) 2023/1781 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 September 2023, the "European Chips Act." The status of integrated production facility or open EU foundry is open for both the installation of a new semiconductor manufacturing facility and the significant scale-up or innovative transformation of an existing semiconductor manufacturing facility, with the Commission's assessment taking into account the readiness of Member States to support the facility and, where relevant, the applicant's overall track record. Critically for materials-stage projects, the Commission has recognised the need for a case-by-case assessment regarding State aid granted to advanced semiconductor production facilities with a view to safeguarding the Union's security of supply and supply-chain resilience while generating significant positive impacts on the wider economy, and the procedures for recognition as an IPF/OEF and for State aid authorisation are conducted in parallel to accelerate decision-making.

The Chips Act establishes two categories of "first-of-a-kind" facility relevant to substrate producers:

- **Integrated Production Facilities (IPFs)** — vertically integrated design-and-manufacture operations (Article 13);
- **Open EU Foundries (OEFs)** — facilities offering production capacity to unrelated undertakings. Open EU foundries are first-of-a-kind facilities for semiconductor manufacturing in the Union that offer production capacity to unrelated undertakings and thereby contribute to the security of supply for the internal market and the resilience of the Union's semiconductor ecosystem, and may, where relevant, contribute to the security of the global semiconductor supply chain. An OEF must demonstrate a clear positive impact, with spill-over effects beyond the individual undertaking or Member State, on the Union's semiconductor value chain in the medium to long term, contributing to security of supply, resilience of the ecosystem, and the growth of start-ups and SMEs.

A merchant substrate supplier — a company that grows bulk crystal and sells finished wafers to multiple unrelated device manufacturers — maps naturally onto the OEF category, since by business-model definition it serves external customers rather than a single captive fab.

Under Article 15(1) of the Chips Act, any undertaking or consortium of undertakings may apply to the Commission for IPF or OEF status, subject to a defined set of criteria, and applicants already engaged in a State aid procedure are encouraged to reference that procedure in their application, since the associated documentation is also useful for the Commission's IPF/OEF assessment. This procedural parallelism is significant for a Polish substrate project: it means a company seeking KRWSIP funding and EU-level IPF/OEF recognition simultaneously can avoid duplicating due-diligence submissions.

Beyond funding, the Chips Act status confers regulatory advantages of direct relevance to capital-intensive crystal-growth operations, which typically require large footprints, high electricity and gas connections, and controlled-atmosphere or high-pressure process equipment: the planning, construction and operation of integrated production facilities and open EU foundries may be considered to be of overriding public interest, provided the remaining statutory conditions are fulfilled, without prejudice to other Union environmental law, and each Member State may designate an authority — with an appointed single-point-of-contact coordinator — to facilitate administrative applications for such facilities. For a facility such as an ammonothermal or HVPE reactor hall, or a Czochralski/vertical-gradient-freeze (VGF) crystal-growth bay, which involves high-pressure autoclaves, ammonia handling, or substantial furnace power draw, this "overriding public interest" designation can materially shorten permitting timelines relative to standard industrial siting.

At the European level, implementation has already produced a meaningful pipeline: the Commission has approved thirteen State aid decisions on first-of-a-kind semiconductor facilities, representing a total public and private investment of over EUR 32 billion, with several further projects still under development. This establishes the precedent and administrative practice that a Polish substrate project could draw on.

### 2.2 Poland's National Framework (KRWSIP)

The National Framework for Supporting Strategic Semiconductor Investments was established on the basis of Regulation (EU) 2023/1781 of 13 September 2023 and the Resolution of the Council of Ministers of 8 December 2023. Proposed by the Ministry of Digital Affairs with an intended budget of $1.5 billion, the programme's objectives cover the entire semiconductor value chain — from design to production — with the goal of supporting projects that develop semiconductor manufacturing in Poland, emphasising investments that enhance EU economic competitiveness, sustainable growth, and job creation. Poland-based reporting quantifies the domestic support pool at over PLN 7 billion, consistent with the roughly $1.5 billion figure cited internationally, covering the 2024–2026 period. The programme was established by the outgoing government in November 2023, with the 2024–2026 funding tranche set at approximately US$1.5 billion, and was explicitly designed to cover the entire value chain from chip design to production.

Eligibility under the Polish framework is anchored directly in the EU-level facility definitions: support under the programme is granted to investors setting up an integrated production plant or an open EU factory on the territory of the Republic of Poland that will conduct activity in the field of semiconductors, with "semiconductor" defined per the European Commission as either an elemental or composite material — including new materials — whose electrical conductivity can be modified, or a component built from layered semiconducting, insulating and conducting materials configured to perform a specific electronic or photonic function. This definition is broad enough to unambiguously encompass bulk crystal boules and finished/epi-ready wafers as "semiconductor" materials in scope, since the material-conductivity clause captures substrate-grade Si, SiC, and GaN directly, independent of whether a device structure has yet been fabricated on it.

### 2.3 The evolving "Policy for the Semiconductor Sector 2025+"

The National Framework's financial instrument is being complemented by a broader strategic document. The Ministry of Digital Affairs has been developing a "Policy for the Semiconductor Sector," a strategic framework aimed at building a robust, innovative and resilient microelectronics ecosystem, with a draft released on 6 February 2025 followed by a public consultation concluding 4 March 2025 in which 38 stakeholders from industry, academia and government submitted 356 suggestions. Consultation participants stressed expanding test and pilot infrastructure, ensuring stable access to utilities and critical raw materials, and establishing a dedicated semiconductor technology park, alongside a forward-looking proposal for cross-border technology microregions such as a Dresden–Wrocław–Prague corridor. Industry submissions to this consultation process argued explicitly for materials-stage prioritization: Poland's semiconductor strategy should focus on the development of technologies, materials and applications where Poland can achieve genuine competitive advantage, taking account of global trends such as AI, IoT and quantum technologies, while concentrating national effort on a selected set of technologies, materials and applications rather than attempting comprehensive coverage. The same industry input called for identifying critical resources and key components whose shortage could disrupt production, and developing mitigation strategies such as supplier diversification, warehousing and domestic manufacturing development, alongside financial and organisational support for companies investing in the production of key components within Poland. These consultation outcomes indicate that raw-material and substrate security is already recognized within Polish policy circles as an under-served priority relative to back-end assembly, which strengthens the case for using KRWSIP allocations deliberately toward substrate-stage projects going forward.

## 3. Poland's Existing Bulk Crystal and Wafer Ecosystem

### 3.1 Ammono — ammonothermal bulk GaN

Poland possesses a genuinely globally distinctive asset in bulk compound-semiconductor crystal growth: Ammono S.A., a Warsaw-based company that pioneered ammonothermal growth of bulk gallium nitride, a technology route pursued by very few organizations worldwide. Ammono grows GaN crystals not via the vapor-deposition approach used by essentially all leading gallium nitride substrate manufacturers, but under high heat and high pressure, in a manner analogous to how the Earth itself forms crystals. The company has built up a stock of high-quality gallium nitride crystals measuring roughly 2 inches (51 mm) at their longest dimension, with plans to slice these into wafer substrates; while 51 mm is a fraction of standard silicon wafer diameters, this size range dominated the market for gallium nitride substrates used chiefly as the foundation for violet laser diodes, a market analysts estimated at upward of US $100 million with continuing double-digit growth. Scaling crystal diameter to 100 mm or beyond was identified as the threshold at which major silicon-industry players would take serious interest in bulk GaN for applications beyond optoelectronics, given GaN's advantages for power and RF devices.

Ammono's technology profile — high structural quality, low dislocation density native GaN — is precisely the profile that the wider bulk-GaN literature identifies as the critical bottleneck for the industry. Only crystallization on high structural quality native seeds will ensure proper progress in bulk GaN development, and new approaches are needed for fabricating gallium nitride crystals and wafers with better control of structural properties and point-defect concentration. Ammonothermal growth, alongside halide vapor phase epitaxy (HVPE) and sodium-flux methods, is one of the three basic bulk-GaN crystal growth technologies, each with distinct advantages and disadvantages, and recent progress in the ammonothermal method — both basic and acidic variants — has yielded high structural quality crystals up to 4 inches in diameter. Ammono's continued scale-up toward larger-diameter, high-quality native GaN seed crystals is directly relevant to the KRWSIP framework's OEF category, since native GaN seed material is a scarce, EU-strategic input that a Polish supplier could offer to unrelated European device and epitaxy companies.

### 3.2 Łukasiewicz – Institute of Microelectronics and Photonics (IMiF)

The other pillar of Poland's substrate-adjacent capability is institutional rather than purely commercial: Łukasiewicz–IMiF, formed from the 2020 merger of two fifty-year-old Polish research institutes. Łukasiewicz–IMiF was created on 1 October 2020 through the consolidation of the Łukasiewicz Institute of Electron Technology (ITE) and the Łukasiewicz Institute of Electronic Materials Technology (ITME), institutes with a fifty-year history, headquartered in Warsaw with additional branches in Piaseczno and Kraków, conducting research in advanced materials, micro- and nanoelectronics, and photonics. The predecessor institute ITME in particular has a long institutional history in bulk crystal growth of compound semiconductors and oxide/fluoride crystals for electro-optic and detector applications, making IMiF the natural R&D anchor for any Polish state effort to expand substrate manufacturing capability.

IMiF is now an explicit participant in EU Chips Act pilot-line infrastructure relevant to wide-bandgap substrates: through implementation of Poland's National Recovery Plan (KPO) and the WGB Pilot Line and INNOSEMI projects under the European Chips Act initiative, the Institute is contributing significantly to the development of the semiconductor sector in Europe, thereby strengthening the technological sovereignty of European industry. Under the Chips Act initiative, Łukasiewicz–IMiF is undertaking an ambitious project to build a modern pilot line for wide-bandgap semiconductors. A wide-bandgap (WBG) pilot line — covering materials such as SiC and GaN — is precisely the kind of shared, pre-competitive infrastructure that lowers the capital barrier for smaller Polish and European firms to develop and qualify new substrate and epitaxial material, complementing (rather than duplicating) KRWSIP's direct capital-expenditure support for commercial-scale facilities.

IMiF's convening role in national policy discussions further indicates its centrality: on 2 April 2026, public consultations on the "Policy for the Semiconductor Sector 2026+" concluded, and Łukasiewicz–IMiF had the honor of hosting a meeting dedicated to rebuilding the national semiconductor industry, with the participation of the Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Digital Affairs, a member of the Sejm and chair of the parliamentary subcommittee on science and higher education, the Minister of Science and Higher Education, and the President of the Łukasiewicz Research Network. Discussions addressed Poland's technological sovereignty challenges — from critical infrastructure and defence to supply-chain continuity and cybersecurity — with the shared conclusion among participants that without domestic competencies in microelectronics there can be no long-term security or development.

### 3.3 Wider market context and adjacent players

Poland's broader semiconductor ecosystem — design houses (Digital Core Design, ChipCraft, Silicon Creations), memory/storage (Solidigm), and the Wrocław back-end cluster anchored by Intel — provides downstream demand pull and workforce depth, but is largely decoupled from bulk crystal growth today. Poland's participation in the European pilot semiconductor production line through UNIPRESS and Łukasiewicz–IMiF showcases the country's growing influence in the European semiconductor ecosystem, with over 30% of market players engaging in partnerships that are driving technological advancement and market consolidation. UNIPRESS (the Institute of High Pressure Physics, Polish Academy of Sciences) is itself a globally recognized centre for high-pressure bulk GaN crystal growth (ammonothermal and high-nitrogen-pressure solution growth), reinforcing that Poland's comparative advantage in the substrate segment is concentrated specifically in nitride materials science rather than in silicon.

By contrast, in the global SiC substrate market that is currently the fastest-growing wafer segment worldwide due to power-electronics and EV demand, Poland has no announced domestic bulk-crystal producer at commercial scale. The SiC wafer market is forecast to grow from USD 0.33 billion in 2022 to USD 1.29 billion by 2029 at a 21.6% CAGR, dominated by players such as Wolfspeed, TankeBlue and SiCrystal GmbH, with most n-type SiC substrates for power semiconductors produced at 6-inch (150 mm) diameter and expected to remain the industry standard for the foreseeable future. This is illustrative of the scale of capital investment already committed by incumbents elsewhere in Europe and Asia: II-VI Incorporated announced a $1 billion, ten-year investment in SiC substrate and epitaxial wafer manufacturing, including expansions in Easton, Pennsylvania and Kista, Sweden, with the Pennsylvania site targeting the equivalent of 1 million 150 mm SiC substrates annually by 2027 to meet demand driven by the electrification of power electronics. Poland's absence from this specific segment represents both a competitive gap and a clear target of opportunity for KRWSIP-supported greenfield investment, discussed further in Section 5.

## 4. How the Framework's Mechanisms Map to Substrate-Manufacturing Economics

Bulk crystal growth and wafer fabrication differ structurally from packaging/assembly or fabless design in ways that determine which KRWSIP/Chips Act mechanisms are most consequential:

| Substrate-manufacturing characteristic | Relevant KRWSIP / Chips Act mechanism |
|---|---|
| Very high capital intensity per unit of output (furnaces, autoclaves, wire saws, CMP lines, epitaxy reactors); long depreciation horizons | Direct capital grants/state aid under the $1.5bn (≈PLN 7bn+) envelope; parallel State-aid authorisation alongside IPF/OEF application to de-risk financing |
| Multi-year qualification cycles before a new substrate source is accepted by device customers | OEF status's emphasis on medium-to-long-term value-chain impact and support for start-up/SME growth, which explicitly tolerates a longer horizon than a single fiscal year |
| Need for guaranteed access to feedstock, high-purity gases (ammonia, silane, HCl), and grid capacity | Ecosystem-coordination role of the Ministry of Digital Affairs and the ministries convened at IMiF (raw-material security explicitly flagged in the 2025+ policy consultation) |
| Large, power- and utility-intensive physical footprint requiring rapid siting and permitting | "Overriding public interest" designation for planning/construction/operation of IPFs and OEFs, plus a single-point-of-contact national coordinating authority |
| Reliance on a small, highly specialized crystal-growth engineering talent pool (not general IC design/process talent) | Institutional anchoring via Łukasiewicz–IMiF/ITME's fifty-year materials R&D base and university partnerships (e.g., Wrocław University of Science and Technology) |
| First-mover/first-of-a-kind status confers strategic leverage disproportionate to firm size (relevant to smaller Polish/EU entrants versus Asian incumbents) | The Chips Act's "first-of-a-kind" facility recognition, open to *both new facilities and significant scale-up of existing ones* — directly applicable to scaling Ammono's ammonothermal GaN process from laboratory/pilot to commercial diameters |
| Need for shared pre-competitive infrastructure (metrology, defect characterization, pilot lines) that no single substrate firm can afford alone | WGB Pilot Line and INNOSEMI projects at Łukasiewicz–IMiF, explicitly framed as Chips Act pillar-1 (production capacity) and pillar-2 (skills/innovation) instruments |

A further structural point specific to Poland: because the National Framework's eligibility criteria are pegged to the Chips Act's technology-neutral definition of "semiconductor" as any material whose conductivity is engineered — rather than to a specific process node or device type — a wide-bandgap or compound-semiconductor bulk-crystal project is *not* disadvantaged relative to a leading-edge Si logic fab in terms of formal eligibility, even though it competes for the same limited pool of capital. This is strategically important because Poland is objectively better positioned in nitride bulk-crystal science (Ammono, UNIPRESS, IMiF) than in leading-edge Si front-end fabrication, where incumbents' capital and IP moats are far larger.

## 5. Gaps, Risks, and Constraints

Several structural issues are not resolved by the framework as currently designed, and should be treated as first-order risks for any substrate-manufacturing strategy built around it:

1. **Scale of the funding envelope relative to substrate-fab capital costs.** A single modern 150–200 mm SiC boule-growth-and-wafering line, or a competitive-scale GaN ammonothermal facility, can require capital expenditure in the hundreds of millions to low billions of euros — a meaningful fraction of, or in excess of, Poland's entire $1.5 billion 2024–2026 envelope if state aid is expected to cover a substantial share. The framework's funding must therefore be understood as *catalytic co-investment* alongside private capital and EU-level Chips for Europe / Chips JU instruments, not as a standalone financing solution.

2. **Energy cost and grid capacity.** Crystal growth (particularly high-pressure ammonothermal growth and Czochralski/VGF processes) is energy-intensive and requires stable, high-capacity electricity supply. Poland's industrial electricity price and grid-connection queue length are material competitiveness variables that sit outside KRWSIP's direct control and depend on parallel energy-policy and grid-investment decisions.

3. **Feedstock and precursor security.** High-purity source materials — polysilicon, high-purity gallium and ammonia, SiC powder — are themselves subject to global supply concentration (much of it outside the EU). The 2025+ policy consultation's emphasis on identifying critical resources and diversifying suppliers is a necessary complement to KRWSIP capital support, since a well-funded wafer fab with unreliable feedstock access is not competitive.

4. **Talent pipeline specificity.** Bulk crystal growth requires expertise (melt/solution thermodynamics, defect engineering, high-pressure process control) that is narrower and less transferable than general semiconductor-device or IC-design skills. Poland's strong STEM base supports the latter more directly than the former; sustained investment in crystal-growth-specific graduate training (in partnership with IMiF/UNIPRESS and universities) is required to avoid a talent bottleneck as any commercial-scale facility ramps.

5. **Qualification risk and time-to-revenue.** New substrate suppliers typically face multi-year device-customer qualification cycles before achieving meaningful revenue, which is a poor match for financing instruments structured around short political/budget cycles (the current KRWSIP tranche is explicitly bounded to 2024–2026). Continuity of support into a successor instrument — which the "Policy for the Semiconductor Sector 2025+" appears designed to provide — is important to avoid a funding cliff mid-qualification.

6. **Segment concentration risk.** Poland's demonstrated substrate strength (nitride bulk crystal growth) is a narrow, high-value niche rather than a broad-based wafer industry; it does not by itself address Europe's larger silicon-wafer or SiC-substrate import dependence. A deliberate choice is required as to whether Poland's national strategy doubles down on its GaN/nitride niche (where it has genuine, defensible technological leadership) versus attempting to enter the far more capital- and incumbent-dominated SiC or Si substrate markets from a standing start.

## 6. Policy Recommendations

Building on the framework's existing architecture and the consultation input already gathered by the Ministry of Digital Affairs, the following actions would strengthen Poland's substrate-manufacturing competitiveness:

1. **Ring-fence or signal a substrate/materials allocation within KRWSIP successor funding**, explicitly naming bulk crystal growth and wafering as a priority sub-category, given that back-end assembly (Intel Wrocław) has already captured the largest single announced investment and risks crowding out smaller, longer-horizon materials-stage applicants in a competitive allocation process.

2. **Use OEF status deliberately to position Polish nitride bulk-crystal capacity as shared EU infrastructure**, since Ammono- or UNIPRESS-scale native GaN seed production serving multiple unrelated European epitaxy/device customers is a textbook fit for the Open EU Foundry category and its associated spill-over/SME-support criteria — likely a stronger fit than IPF status, which presumes vertically integrated single-owner production.

3. **Pair capital grants with binding feedstock- and energy-security commitments**, translating the 2025+ consultation's raw-material and utility-access recommendations into concrete conditions or co-investments (e.g., grid-connection prioritization, high-purity-gas supply agreements) attached to any substrate-facility state aid award.

4. **Expand the WGB Pilot Line's scope and access rules** so that it functions as genuinely open, pre-competitive infrastructure for defect characterization, wafer qualification, and process scale-up — reducing the capital burden on smaller entrants attempting to scale from lab-scale boules (the ~2-inch to 4-inch range historically demonstrated) toward the 100 mm+ diameters that unlock broader power/RF device markets.

5. **Fund targeted crystal-growth engineering training programs** in partnership with Łukasiewicz–IMiF, UNIPRESS, and technical universities (Warsaw University of Technology, Wrocław University of Science and Technology), distinct from general semiconductor-workforce initiatives, to build the specific talent base that melt- and solution-growth processes require.

6. **Sequence expectations realistically**: treat 2024–2026 KRWSIP funding as a bridge that de-risks private and EU co-investment for materials-stage projects with multi-year qualification cycles, and ensure the "Policy for the Semiconductor Sector 2025+" (or 2026+) explicitly commits to funding continuity for substrate-stage projects that will not reach commercial revenue within a single budget cycle.

7. **Make an explicit strategic choice on segment focus**, consistent with industry advice already submitted to the Ministry: concentrate scarce state-aid capacity on the wide-bandgap/nitride niche where Poland holds genuine, internationally recognized technological leadership (ammonothermal GaN), rather than diffusing limited resources across silicon or SiC segments where global incumbents' capital scale is an order of magnitude larger.

## 7. Conclusion

Poland's National Framework for Supporting Strategic Semiconductor Investments, operating within the legal architecture of the EU Chips Act, provides a genuinely applicable — if not yet fully exploited — instrument for supporting bulk crystal and wafer manufacturing. Its technology-neutral eligibility definition, its parallel-track state-aid/IPF-OEF application process, its "overriding public interest" permitting designation, and its explicit inclusion of the full value chain "from design to production" all extend, by design, to substrate-stage activity. Poland additionally holds a distinctive, defensible technological asset in ammonothermal bulk GaN crystal growth (Ammono, UNIPRESS) and an institutional R&D anchor with a fifty-year materials-science history (Łukasiewicz–IMiF, successor to ITME/ITE) already participating in EU Chips Act pilot-line and skills projects. The principal risks to converting this position into durable competitiveness are not legal or programmatic but structural: the scale of the funding envelope relative to substrate-fab capital costs, energy and feedstock security, a narrow specialist talent pool, and financing horizons misaligned with multi-year customer-qualification cycles. The emerging "Policy for the Semiconductor Sector 2025+," if it acts on the materials- and raw-resource-security recommendations already surfaced in public consultation, offers a credible path to addressing these gaps and to positioning Poland — realistically, in its nitride/wide-bandgap niche rather than across the full substrate market — as a meaningful contributor to European semiconductor materials sovereignty.
