# Nobuyuki Imaishi (今石 宣之) — Scientific Biography

**Institute of Advanced Material Study / Institute for Materials Chemistry and Engineering (IMCE), Kyushu University**

## Overview

Nobuyuki Imaishi is a Japanese chemical engineer and fluid dynamicist whose career-long research program has centered on interfacial transport phenomena — particularly Marangoni (surface-tension-driven) convection — in contexts ranging from classical chemical-engineering mass transfer to microgravity fluid physics and, most extensively, the numerical modelling of melt flows in crystal growth. Based for most of his career at Kyushu University, first at the Research Institute for Applied Mechanics / Institute of Advanced Material Study and later at the Institute for Materials Chemistry and Engineering (IMCE), he built one of Japan's principal research groups on thermocapillary and buoyancy-driven convection in half-zone liquid bridges, annular pools, and Czochralski/floating-zone melt configurations, and became a long-standing collaborator in Japan's microgravity fluid-physics program (JAXA/NASDA "Kibo" experiments) as well as in international silicon-crystal-growth modelling consortia.

## Institutional and disciplinary trajectory

Imaishi's training and early career were in chemical engineering, with a specific focus on interfacial and mass-transfer phenomena at fluid–fluid boundaries — a subfield concerned with how surface tension gradients, interfacial turbulence, and mass transfer interact at liquid–liquid and liquid–gas interfaces. His earliest identifiable publications, from the mid-to-late 1970s, addressed the effect of interfacial turbulence on mass transfer enhancement, a classical problem in extraction and absorption engineering, worked on jointly with M. Hozawa and K. Fujinawa (published in Kagaku Kogaku Ronbunshu, the journal of the Society of Chemical Engineers, Japan). This established the conceptual throughline of his subsequent forty-plus years of work: the coupling between interfacial (Marangoni) stresses and bulk fluid motion.

By the 1980s and 1990s Imaishi's affiliation was with Kyushu University's **Institute of Advanced Material Study** (九州大学機能物質科学研究所, "Institute for Fundamental Research of Organic Chemistry" / later reorganized), located on the Kasuga campus. In this period, his research shifted decisively from classical chemical-engineering interfacial mass transfer toward the fluid mechanics of Marangoni convection proper — its stability theory, its behavior in reduced gravity, and its role in materials processing. This institute was later absorbed into and continued as the **Institute for Materials Chemistry and Engineering (IMCE)** at Kyushu University, under which much of his later-career (2000s–2010s) output is affiliated.

Across his career Imaishi held the rank of professor and served as a principal investigator on numerous microgravity and crystal-growth-related research programs, supervising graduate students and postdoctoral researchers (a lineage that includes collaborators such as Shoichi Yasuhiro, You-Rong Li, Wanyuan Shi, and Xuan/Xin Liu, among others) who went on to independent research careers, several of them explicitly building on the melt-convection numerical methodology developed in his group.

## Research themes

### 1. Interfacial mass transfer and interfacial turbulence (1970s)

Imaishi's earliest published work, done in the chemical-engineering tradition, examined how instabilities at a liquid–liquid interface (interfacial turbulence driven by Marangoni-type surface-tension gradients during mass transfer of surface-active solutes) enhance mass-transfer rates beyond what quiescent-interface film theory predicts. This work proposed an expression for the mass-transfer enhancement factor attributable to interfacial turbulence, situating Imaishi from the outset within the broader tradition of Marangoni-effect research in unit operations (extraction, absorption, distillation).

### 2. Marangoni convection: fundamentals, stability, and reviews

Over the following decades Imaishi became one of Japan's principal expositors of Marangoni-effect physics for the materials-engineering community. His widely cited Japanese-language review "Marangoni Convections under Microgravity Field" (微小重力環境でのマランゴニ対流), published in *Materia Japan* (the bulletin of the Japan Institute of Metals), surveyed:

- the stability of two-fluid layers subject to combined Rayleigh–Bénard and Marangoni instabilities;
- thermocapillary effects on the behavior of Czochralski (CZ) single-crystal pullers;
- conditions for the onset of interfacial turbulence during mass transfer of surface-active solutes;
- surface acceleration/stagnation phenomena on surfactant-solution surfaces and their relation to interfacial contamination.

A companion review, "流体-流体界面現象とマランゴニ効果" ("Fluid–Fluid Interfacial Phenomena and the Marangoni Effect"), extended this synthesis, distinguishing thermal, solutal, and surfactant-monolayer-driven Marangoni effects as three mechanistically distinct classes — a taxonomy that recurs throughout his later technical papers.

### 3. Microgravity fluid physics and liquid bridges

A defining strand of Imaishi's career was participation in Japan's microgravity materials-science program, in which thermocapillary (Marangoni) convection in half-zone liquid bridges — a laboratory idealization of the floating-zone crystal growth configuration — was studied both computationally and via space-based/drop-tower/parabolic-flight experiments, since terrestrial buoyancy convection masks the pure thermocapillary flow at the length scales of interest.

Representative and characteristic contributions include:

- Numerical simulation of **oscillatory axisymmetric Marangoni convection in small liquid bridges** (with Shoichi Yasuhiro), constructing stability diagrams for the critical Marangoni number governing the transition from steady to oscillatory flow, for fluids of moderate Prandtl number — published in the Institute of Advanced Material Study's own bulletin (1994).
- Later work (into the 2000s–2010s) extending this liquid-bridge program to **three-dimensional, non-axisymmetric instabilities**, hydrothermal-wave onset, and the influence of ambient gas flow and heat transfer at the free surface on the instability and oscillation mode of Marangoni convection — this line culminating in collaborative work with Y. Kamotani, S. Matsumoto, K. Nishino, I. Ueno, and A. Komiya, presented at international symposia on physical sciences in space (e.g. ISPS/ITTW conferences) and tied to actual microgravity experiments flown aboard the International Space Station's Kibo module.
- Investigations of hydrothermal waves and rotating/oscillatory convection in **shallow annular pools** of model fluids (typically silicone oil), a geometry chosen as an idealization of the melt pool in Czochralski growth, in collaborated papers with Wanyuan Shi and Michael K. Ermakov published in the *Journal of Crystal Growth* (2006), examining the effect of pool rotation on thermocapillary convection and the character of hydrothermal waves in differentially heated shallow annular pools.

### 4. Melt convection and instability in crystal growth (Czochralski, floating zone)

The largest and most sustained body of Imaishi's output concerns the **numerical modelling of buoyancy- and Marangoni-driven convection in melt crystal growth**, with particular emphasis on silicon melts (low-Prandtl-number fluids relevant to the semiconductor industry) and oxide/silicon annular-pool configurations relevant to Czochralski (CZ) growth.

Key threads include:

- **Three-dimensional oscillatory flow instabilities** in thin annular pools of silicon melt (work with Y.-R. Li, T. Azami, and T. Hibiya, *Journal of Crystal Growth*, 2004), characterizing the transition to time-dependent, azimuthally structured convection relevant to striation formation and dopant-segregation non-uniformity in grown crystals.
- Thermocapillary and buoyancy-driven flow instability analyses in configurations directly modelling the CZ puller free-surface melt zone, connecting flow-regime transitions to observable crystal-quality defects (oxygen and dopant striations).
- Continued collaboration into the 2000s–2010s with a network of crystal-growth fluid-dynamics researchers (including collaborators publishing from Kyushu, Tohoku, and Nagoya universities) extending this melt-convection modelling to floating-zone and related melt-growth geometries, generally combining finite-difference/finite-volume Navier–Stokes solvers with energy-transport and, in some studies, Marangoni/surfactant boundary conditions at the free melt surface.

This body of work situates Imaishi within the international community of crystal-growth transport-phenomena modellers (alongside figures such as R.A. Brown, S. Kou, K. Kakimoto, D. Schwabe, V.V. Kalaev, and T. Tsukada), with his own distinguishing contribution being a particular emphasis on the free-surface (Marangoni) boundary condition and on annular-pool/liquid-bridge idealized geometries as a route to isolating instability mechanisms before they are embedded in full industrial-scale CZ process models.

## Scientific character and legacy

Three features mark Imaishi's scientific profile as distinct within the broader crystal-growth-modelling community:

1. **Disciplinary origin in interfacial chemical engineering.** Unlike many crystal-growth modellers who arrive from materials science or applied mathematics, Imaishi's grounding in classical interfacial mass-transfer engineering gave his later fluid-dynamics work a persistent attentiveness to the physical chemistry of the free surface itself (surfactant effects, interfacial turbulence, surface contamination) rather than treating the free-surface Marangoni boundary condition as a purely mathematical closure.

2. **Bridge between idealized fluid-physics experiments and applied crystal growth.** His research consistently used simplified, well-controlled geometries — the half-zone liquid bridge and the shallow annular pool — as physical model systems standing in for the free-surface melt zone of real Czochralski and floating-zone crystal pullers, allowing instability mechanisms (oscillatory thermocapillary flow, hydrothermal waves) to be characterized cleanly before their consequences for crystal quality were assessed.

3. **Sustained engagement with microgravity science.** Because buoyancy convection obscures pure thermocapillary flow at laboratory scale, much of the liquid-bridge instability work in Imaishi's group was designed in explicit dialogue with, and partly validated against, microgravity experiments conducted under Japan's space-based materials-science program, linking his university-based numerical/laboratory research directly to JAXA experimental campaigns.

His body of published work — spanning Japanese-language engineering-society journals (Kagaku Kogaku Ronbunshu, Materia Japan), the institute's own bulletin (九州大学機能物質科学研究所報告), and international venues (*Journal of Crystal Growth*, *Journal of Fluid Mechanics*-adjacent literature via citing works, and international microgravity/space-science symposia) — reflects a career built primarily within Japan's crystal-growth and microgravity-science communities, with strong integration into international collaborative networks on melt-convection instability rather than a single-author "school" model of scientific production.
