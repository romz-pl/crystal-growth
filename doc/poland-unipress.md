# The Institute of High Pressure Physics, PAS (UNIPRESS): Role, Capabilities, and Prospects as an Anchor Partner for a Polish Compound-Semiconductor Ecosystem

## Executive Summary

The Institute of High Pressure Physics, Polish Academy of Sciences (IHPP PAS), known internationally as **UNIPRESS**, is a 50-plus-year-old research institute in Warsaw that holds a globally recognized, technologically unique position in bulk crystal growth of gallium nitride (GaN). Its high-pressure solution growth (HPSG) technology, developed under the late Prof. Sylwester Porowski, produced the lowest-dislocation-density GaN crystals in the world and underpinned decades of nitride semiconductor physics. Through its spin-off and later reabsorbed subsidiaries — TopGaN and Ammono S.A. (now operated internally as the "NL-3" pilot line) — UNIPRESS has extended this scientific leadership into ammonothermal and hybrid HVPE-ammonothermal GaN substrate production, positioning it among a small global cohort of ~2-inch ammonothermal GaN suppliers alongside SixPoint Materials (US) and Mitsubishi Chemical/SCIOCS (Japan).

UNIPRESS is not, however, a volume semiconductor manufacturer. It is a materials-science research institute with pilot-scale production capability, an academic culture, modest headcount (~240 staff), and revenue in the low tens of millions of USD. Its core strength — deep mastery of thermodynamics, high-pressure/high-temperature crystal growth physics, and defect engineering in GaN — is precisely the kind of capability that is scarce, slow to build, and highly valuable as the anchor of a national compound-semiconductor materials strategy. Its core weakness — the absence of industrial-scale wafer manufacturing, large-diameter (6–8 inch) process capability, and a balance sheet suited to capital-intensive fab-adjacent investment — means it cannot by itself become a vertically integrated wafer producer at competitive volumes.

This report concludes that UNIPRESS is a **necessary but not sufficient** partner for establishing an advanced compound-semiconductor crystal growth and wafer ecosystem in Poland. It is best positioned as the materials-IP and pilot-line anchor of a consortium that also includes CEZAMAT (device integration and cleanroom infrastructure), the Łukasiewicz Research Network's Institute of Microelectronics and Photonics (IMiF, packaging/epitaxy/testing), industrial capital partners, and access to EU Chips Act and Chips Joint Undertaking financing mechanisms. Scaling UNIPRESS's technology to competitive wafer diameters (4–6 inch) and commercial volumes will require capital investment on the order of tens to low hundreds of millions of euros, a multi-year timeline, and deliberate governance to preserve the institute's research culture while professionalizing its manufacturing operations.

## 1. Institutional Profile

### 1.1 Origins and Mission

UNIPRESS was founded in 1972 as an outgrowth of the Laboratory of Pressure Studies of Semiconductors at the Institute of Physics, PAS, itself rooted in the "Leonard Sosnowski School of Semiconductors" — one of the foundational lineages of Polish solid-state physics.UNIPRESS was created 50 years ago (1972) from the Laboratory of Pressure Studies of Semiconductors of the Institute of Physics PAS, building on an exceptionally strong foundation based on the Leonard Sosnowski School of Semiconductors, supported by excellent foreign cooperation. Its founding director, Prof. Sylwester Porowski, is broadly credited within the Polish physics community with building the institute's original high-pressure experimental infrastructure from scratch and establishing its international scientific reputation.

The institute's traditional research program spans several adjacent domains beyond nitride semiconductors, including biomaterials and protein biophysics under pressure, soft matter and glass science, superconductors and hydrogen storage technologies, and terahertz radiation physics. This diversification is relevant to any partnership assessment: UNIPRESS is not a single-product organization, and its semiconductor activities compete internally for institutional attention and investment against these other mature research lines.

### 1.2 Organizational Footprint

UNIPRESS operates across several physical sites in the Warsaw area, each specialized: the main headquarters on Sokołowska Street houses GaN crystal growth using the HVPE method and ultra-high pressure annealing (UHPA); the Prymasa Tysiąclecia Avenue site contains research laboratories including cleanrooms for semiconductors and biomaterials; a CEZAMAT terahertz radiation laboratory operates at the Warsaw University of Technology's Poleczki Street site; ammonothermal GaN crystal growth and substrate production takes place at Stanisławów Pierwszy; and the Unipress Innovation Park at Lasek, in Celestynów municipality, hosts hydroextrusion and high-pressure hydrogen storage technologies.

Institutionally, the GaN crystal growth infrastructure has been formally recognized on the Polish Roadmap for Research Infrastructures as the "GaN UNIPRESS" Centre for Nitride Semiconductor Physics and Technology. This designation matters for partnership evaluation: it signals that the Polish state already treats UNIPRESS's GaN infrastructure as strategic national capability, which is a favorable precondition for further public co-investment.

UNIPRESS is authorized to confer doctoral and postdoctoral degrees and employs over 240 people, spanning crystal growth, MBE epitaxy, superconductors and hydrogen technologies, plastic deformation under high pressure, and other groups. According to third-party business intelligence aggregation, the institute's annual revenue was on the order of $13.5 million in 2026 — a figure consistent with a research-institute-scale organization rather than an industrial wafer fab, and useful context for assessing what UNIPRESS can and cannot self-finance.

### 1.3 Leadership and Technical Continuity

Current leadership of the crystal growth program includes Prof. Michał Bockowski, who received his M.Sc. Eng. in Solid State Physics from the Warsaw University of Technology, his Ph.D. in the Chemistry of Solids from the University of Montpellier II, France, in 1995, and his habilitation from the Institute of Physics PAS in 2013, and who served as vice president at TopGaN Ltd. from 2008 to 2015 before becoming professor and head of the Crystal Growth Laboratory at IHPP PAS, as well as a visiting professor at Nagoya University's CIRFE IMaSS. This career trajectory — academic training, direct industrial spin-off leadership experience, and ongoing international academic collaboration (Nagoya, a major center of nitride semiconductor research) — is representative of the institute's broader model of research-to-industry personnel flow and is a genuine asset for partnership design, since it means UNIPRESS's senior technical staff already have first-hand experience operating a commercial GaN entity.

## 2. Technical Contributions to Semiconductor Crystal Growth

### 2.1 High-Pressure Solution Growth of GaN

UNIPRESS's most consequential scientific contribution is the high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) solution growth technique for bulk GaN, developed from the 1990s onward. Since more than 20 years, UNIPRESS's scientists have worked intensively on the physics and technology of GaN and related semiconductors, research that led to the creation of a unique technology of high-pressure growth of GaN crystals with dislocation density much lower than crystals grown at atmospheric pressure.

The physical rationale is instructive for evaluating the institute's core scientific asset: nitrogen has a strong tendency to escape GaN at typical growth temperatures because the equilibrium N₂ partial pressure over molten Ga-N solutions rises steeply with temperature. Suppressing this decomposition requires imposing very high external N₂ pressure (of order 1–2 GPa) during growth from a Ga melt — a regime few laboratories worldwide can access with the necessary precision. UNIPRESS's decades of high-pressure apparatus engineering (originally developed for fundamental semiconductor physics under pressure, not for crystal growth per se) gave it a rare pre-existing capability base from which to attack this problem. The institute's related work on the pressure dependence of the GaN melting point addresses a genuinely open thermodynamic question: a long-standing puzzle regarding GaN decomposition and melting under high pressure and high temperature, including the possibility of a consistent parameterization of the pressure evolution of melting temperature across basic semiconductors such as GaN, germanium, and silicon.

The practical outcome of this approach — crystals with dislocation densities as low as ~10²–10⁴ cm⁻² — is dramatically better than the 10⁸–10¹² cm⁻² typical of heteroepitaxial GaN-on-sapphire or GaN-on-Si films, but the method is fundamentally limited in scale: one growth run can produce 20–30 crystals of roughly 10 mm diameter, and the technique is explicitly not suited to volume production of millions of wafers but rather to special, high-value devices such as high-power laser diodes. This scale ceiling is the central technical reason UNIPRESS's flagship HPHT method, on its own, cannot be the basis of a volume wafer-manufacturing business, and is a key input to the partnership strategy discussed in Section 5.

### 2.2 Ammonothermal and Hybrid HVPE-Ammonothermal Growth

Recognizing the diameter limitation of HPHT growth, UNIPRESS pursued a complementary and more scalable route: ammonothermal crystallization (an analogue of hydrothermal quartz growth, using supercritical ammonia as the transport solvent with a mineralizer to enhance GaN solubility), often combined with hydride vapor-phase epitaxy (HVPE) for thickening. This work was carried out primarily through **Ammono S.A.**, a Warsaw-based commercial entity working in close partnership with, and ultimately absorbed into, UNIPRESS.

Ammono and UNIPRESS developed proprietary hybrid Ammono-HVPE-Ammono technology enabling high-volume, high-quality GaN seed replication, first presented at the SPIE Photonics West Gallium Nitride Materials and Devices IX conference in 2014, with a threading dislocation density of 2×10⁵ cm⁻² and average FWHM of 19 arcsec placing the material among the top class of existing GaN manufacturing approaches. A related grant-funded result showed that using ammonothermally grown GaN as a seed, smooth HVPE-GaN layers up to 2.5 mm thick could be crystallized at a stable growth rate of 240 μm/h with excellent crystalline quality — no cracks and a low threading dislocation density of 5×10⁴ cm⁻² — and then sliced into wafers, with ammonothermal-GaN dislocation density on the order of 10⁴ cm⁻², at least two orders of magnitude better than competing technologies for that metric at the time.

The commercial history of this line of work is directly relevant to partnership due diligence: on 8 January 2019, IHPP acquired Ammono S.A. out of bankruptcy, absorbing the company's commercial activity as an internal pilot line, now delivering GaN substrates on-demand. This bankruptcy-and-reabsorption history is an important cautionary data point — it indicates that even with strong underlying materials science, Ammono as a stand-alone commercial venture could not sustain itself financially, likely due to insufficient volume, working-capital constraints, or an underdeveloped downstream customer base in Poland/Europe. Today this activity operates as **NL-3** within IHPP, and independent literature places UNIPRESS/NL-3 among the small set of serious global players in 2-inch ammonothermal GaN: within the nascent ammonothermal GaN market, the strongest commercial contenders in roughly 2-inch wafers include NL-3 (from IHPP, incorporating what was formerly Ammono), SixPoint, Kyocera (formerly Soraa), and Mitsubishi Chemical Corp.

### 2.3 Substrate Quality Metrics and Remaining Technical Barriers

Peer-reviewed materials-science literature co-authored by UNIPRESS researchers is candid about both progress and remaining barriers. Crystallographic flatness (radius of curvature) is a key differentiator between growth methods: for a 2-inch GaN wafer, the required radius of curvature should exceed roughly 15 m, whereas commercial HVPE-GaN substrates typically achieve only 5–10 m; sodium-flux and ammonothermal GaN can meet the more demanding requirement, but no systematic comparison yet exists of device yield on bent versus flat 2-inch substrates because sufficiently high-structural-quality 2-inch GaN wafers remain scarce on the market. Surface preparation know-how at IHPP is documented directly: an epi-ready (0001) surface — free of subsurface damage, with RMS roughness below 0.1 nm — has been demonstrated on 2-inch ammonothermal GaN wafers fabricated at IHPP PAS, characterized by atomic force microscopy.

The field-wide consensus, including from UNIPRESS-affiliated authors, is that the primary technical hurdles between ammonothermal GaN and widespread commercialization remain scaling to larger wafer diameters, improving wafer throughput and yield, and maintaining or improving bulk crystal quality at scale. This is the honest technical summary a prospective partner needs: UNIPRESS possesses genuine, internationally competitive materials science and pilot-scale process know-how, but — like the rest of the ammonothermal GaN field globally, not merely UNIPRESS specifically — has not yet solved the scale-up problem that separates a research pilot line from an industrial wafer fab.

### 2.4 Complementary Nitride Device and Characterization Capability

Beyond bulk crystal growth, UNIPRESS maintains device-relevant epitaxy and characterization infrastructure. Its molecular-beam epitaxy (MBE) laboratory works on blue and green light-emitting diodes and laser diodes using plasma-assisted molecular beam epitaxy, long-wavelength light emitters, structure-design optimization of GaN-based devices, crystal growth mechanisms on GaN surfaces of varying polarity and crystallographic orientation, and vertical n-p-n GaN/InGaN/GaN heterojunction transistors fabricated on native GaN substrates via MBE. This vertically-integrated substrate-to-device research chain (bulk crystal → wafer → epitaxial device layer → transistor/laser structure) is a genuine differentiator versus institutions that only supply substrates or only do device epitaxy — it means UNIPRESS scientists understand the full value chain and can credibly evaluate whether a given substrate improvement will actually matter at the device level.

## 3. Economic and Market Context

### 3.1 Global GaN Substrate/Wafer Market Sizing

Multiple independent market-research firms currently size the global GaN substrate and wafer market in the low-to-mid single-digit billions of USD, with a wide range reflecting methodological differences and market-segment boundaries (bare substrate vs. epiwafer, native GaN vs. GaN-on-foreign-substrate). Representative estimates: the GaN Substrate and GaN Wafer Market was estimated at $1.42 billion in 2024, forecast to reach $6.85 billion by 2033 at an 18.9% CAGR, with 6-inch GaN wafers dominating volume and 8-inch GaN substrates the high-growth premium segment. A separate estimate places the GaN Substrate and GaN Wafer Market at $2.8 billion in 2025, rising to $7.6 billion by 2034 at an 11.7% CAGR, while the broader GaN semiconductor *device* market (substrates plus devices) is estimated at roughly $4.83 billion in 2026, growing to $10.55 billion by 2031 at 16.92% CAGR, with GaN-on-SiC holding 59.74% share in 2025 and GaN-on-Si the fastest-growing substrate technology at 40.09% CAGR.

The narrower *native/bulk* GaN substrate segment relevant to UNIPRESS's core competence — as opposed to hetero-substrates like GaN-on-Si or GaN-on-SiC used for most commodity power/RF devices — is considerably smaller and higher-margin, serving applications (laser diodes, high-power vertical transistors, high-brightness LEDs, and micro-LED) that specifically require the low defect density only native bulk GaN can provide. This is UNIPRESS's actual addressable niche, not the broader GaN device market.

### 3.2 Wafer-Diameter Economics

Diameter scaling is the dominant cost lever in this industry, and the literature is explicit about the magnitude: moving from 4-inch to 6-inch free-standing GaN wafers reduces cost by roughly 60% because every wafer offers more die sites while maintaining similar processing cycles, and rapid scale-up of 6-inch HVPE free-standing GaN production is lowering cost per cm² industry-wide. Capital intensity rises correspondingly: capital requirements intensify with each diameter increase, as new autoclaves, crucibles, and wafer-handling robots raise entry barriers and tend to consolidate supply among well-funded incumbents, even as competitive pressure from 300 mm silicon carbide pushes suppliers toward 8-inch GaN capability. Government-backed capacity examples illustrate the scale of capital typically required to establish a single competitive 6-inch line: MACOM's federally backed 6-inch GaN-on-SiC line exemplifies how public incentives anchor current diameter standards.

This diameter economics is the crux of the industrial challenge for UNIPRESS: its flagship HPHT technology tops out around 2-inch equivalent crystal diameter per growth run in small multiples, and even its more scalable ammonothermal/hybrid HVPE line is, per the market literature above, positioned in the 2-inch commercial tier — one to two full diameter generations behind where the volume market (4–6 inch, moving to 8-inch) is heading. A Poland-based ecosystem built around UNIPRESS's *current* process scale would be competing in a shrinking, premium niche rather than the growing commodity volume segment, unless substantial process-scale-up investment is made.

### 3.3 Competitive Landscape

Independent market analyses consistently list UNIPRESS (referenced variously as "Unipress Ltd" or via its NL-3/Ammono lineage) alongside global GaN substrate suppliers such as Saint-Gobain, Sumitomo Electric Industries, Toshiba, Soitec, Mitsubishi Chemical, Kyma Technologies, Fujitsu, Aixtron, EpiGaN, NTT Advanced Technology, NGK Insulators, and PAM-Xiamen, among others. Being named in this competitive set at all is meaningful for a small research institute, but the list also underscores the scale mismatch: nearly every other entity listed is either a large diversified materials conglomerate (Sumitomo, Toshiba, Mitsubishi Chemical, NGK, Saint-Gobain) or a well-capitalized specialist with substantial external investment (Soitec, Kyma). UNIPRESS is, by a wide margin, the smallest and most academically embedded organization on this list.

Historical industry commentary on TopGaN, UNIPRESS's earlier laser-diode-focused spin-off, corroborates the niche positioning: TopGaN, founded in 2001 as a spin-off from UNIPRESS, employed about 20 people and retained an academic outlook, with universities forming the majority of its customers; the growth technique was explicitly characterized by TopGaN's own vice-president as not a growth technique for millions of wafers, but for special devices, and TopGaN was unable to extend the growth technique to 2-inch material. This is a useful honest benchmark of scale for evaluating what "UNIPRESS-derived" commercial entities have historically achieved without external capital infusion.

### 3.4 Poland's Broader Semiconductor Policy Context

UNIPRESS's relevance to a national ecosystem strategy must be read against Poland's evolving national semiconductor policy and the wider European Chips Act framework. Poland has recently adopted formal strategic positioning: Poland now has a national semiconductor strategy backed by the government, combined with growing engagement from organizations like SEMI, and industry observers have called Poland's national chip strategy both timely and critical for creating a conducive environment through clear roadmaps, strong government backing, and industry alignment, while noting that Poland had not fully capitalized on the initial EU Chips Act.

A Polish Investment and Trade Agency (PAIH) assessment explicitly names UNIPRESS as one of three institutions expected to carry technology-transfer responsibility in the national strategy: IMiF, Unipress, and CEZAMAT are involved in European research and ensure technology transfer, addressing a historical gap — cooperation between higher-education institutes and industry — that was missing in many previous waves of Polish industrialization; to capitalize on the current opportunity, the report recommends Poland invest in back-end capacity such as chip assembly, testing, and packaging, positioning Poland as a strategic semiconductor hub of Europe. Context on existing large investments underscores that Poland's semiconductor gravity currently sits in packaging/testing/back-end and design/R&D centers rather than front-end wafer fabrication or compound-semiconductor crystal growth: Intel operates an R&D center in Gdańsk, one of the largest in Europe, and is constructing an assembly-and-test facility in Wrocław with a scheduled 2027 operations start expected to create 2,000 direct jobs; Kraków-based Amkor Technology is expanding its outsourced semiconductor assembly and test operations; and a Kearney consultancy report identifies Poland as a pivotal player especially in the back-end segment of packaging, testing, and assembly. Separately, Intel is investing $4.6 billion in the Polish assembly-and-test facility, which will test chips fabricated in Germany and Ireland, with the Polish government expected to cover roughly one-third of the investment.

On the R&D-infrastructure side, CEZAMAT (Centre for Advanced Materials and Technology, Warsaw University of Technology) is Poland's most prominent EU-recognized pilot-line participant: CEZAMAT is one of four pilot lines within the FAMES Consortium, one of the largest projects under the European Chips Act with EUR 830 million in funding from participating member states and the Chips Joint Undertaking, developing technologies such as FD-SOI at 10 nm and 7 nm nodes plus embedded non-volatile memories, accessible to universities and companies across Europe; CEZAMAT's consortium, led by Warsaw University of Technology, also includes Poland's Military University of Technology, the University of Warsaw, and various Polish Academy of Sciences institutes, and its Intelligent Semiconductor Systems Department has teams in both planar semiconductor technologies and integrated photonics. Notably, this FAMES/CEZAMAT pilot-line activity is silicon-CMOS-oriented (FD-SOI), not compound-semiconductor-oriented — meaning it is complementary to, rather than overlapping with, a UNIPRESS-anchored compound-semiconductor materials line, and the two could in principle be linked (UNIPRESS supplying advanced substrates, CEZAMAT/FAMES providing device processing infrastructure) without competing for the same EU funding envelope.

At the pan-European policy level, funding mechanisms exist that a Polish ecosystem could plausibly access: the European Chips Act, strengthened by the Chips Act 2.0 proposal introduced by the Commission in June 2026, supports large-scale technological capacity building through the Chips Joint Undertaking, which funds five pilot lines for process development, test, experimentation, and small-scale production, aiming to double the EU's global semiconductor market share to 20% by the end of the decade. Nine member states, including Poland, have also signed onto a coordination mechanism: on 12 March 2025, Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Poland, Spain, and the Netherlands launched the Semiconductor Coalition, agreeing to reinforce cooperation to strengthen Europe's competitiveness and strategic autonomy in the semiconductor sector through research support, capacity expansion, and workforce development, a move the European Commission welcomed as a strong signal of member-state commitment.

## 4. SWOT Assessment of UNIPRESS as an Ecosystem Partner

**Strengths**
- Globally unique, deeply defensible IP and process know-how in high-pressure GaN crystal growth, developed over 50+ years and validated in peer-reviewed literature and international conferences (SPIE Photonics West).
- Demonstrated ability to achieve dislocation densities and epi-ready surface quality competitive with, or better than, the best commercial alternatives at small diameters.
- Full-chain internal expertise from bulk crystal growth through MBE device epitaxy, enabling substrate-development decisions to be informed by real device performance feedback.
- Already formally recognized as strategic national infrastructure (Polish Roadmap for Research Infrastructures) and explicitly named in Poland's national semiconductor strategy documentation alongside CEZAMAT and IMiF.
- Existing commercial delivery channel (NL-3 pilot line) for on-demand GaN substrate sales, meaning the organization already has *some* institutional experience with customer-facing commercial operations, contracts, and export sales.
- Doctoral/postdoctoral degree-granting authority provides a built-in talent pipeline mechanism.

**Weaknesses**
- Core HPHT process is inherently limited to small-diameter, low-volume output — structurally unsuited to the 4–8 inch volume tier where most market growth and cost-competitiveness now concentrates.
- Prior commercial vehicle (Ammono S.A.) went bankrupt and had to be absorbed back into the institute, indicating that stand-alone commercialization of UNIPRESS technology has already been tried and has not yet succeeded without institutional/state backstopping.
- Modest scale (~240 employees, ~$13.5M annual revenue) relative to the capital intensity required for wafer-diameter scale-up and relative to competitors that are divisions of major diversified conglomerates.
- Academic institutional culture and incentive structures (publication, degree-granting, grant cycles) are not natively aligned with the operational discipline, yield engineering, and delivery-schedule commitments industrial wafer customers require.
- Diversified mission (biomaterials, hydrogen storage, superconductors, THz) means semiconductor crystal growth competes internally for institutional capital, leadership attention, and facility investment.

**Opportunities**
- Growing global native-GaN demand in exactly the premium application segments (laser diodes, vertical power devices, micro-LED) where UNIPRESS's low-defect-density strength is most valuable and least commoditized.
- Poland's national semiconductor strategy and EU Chips Act / Chips Joint Undertaking funding mechanisms, plus the newly formed nine-country Semiconductor Coalition, create a policy and financing window that did not exist during Ammono's earlier commercial struggles.
- CEZAMAT's FAMES pilot-line role is silicon/CMOS-focused, creating a natural, non-competing complementary partnership for combined "compound-semiconductor substrate plus advanced device processing" positioning within Poland's ecosystem.
- Poland's existing back-end strength (Intel assembly/test in Wrocław, Amkor in Kraków) creates a partial downstream customer base and workforce base that a compound-semiconductor materials strategy could eventually feed into, though today these facilities process silicon, not compound semiconductors.
- Growing intra-European urgency around supply-chain sovereignty for critical materials (GaN, SiC) as strategic technologies for defense, telecom infrastructure, and EV power electronics.

**Threats**
- Well-capitalized incumbents (Sumitomo, Mitsubishi Chemical, Toshiba, Kyocera/Soraa, SixPoint) are simultaneously pursuing diameter scale-up, and the capital-intensity of each diameter jump tends toward market consolidation among the best-funded players — a dynamic that could leave a slow-to-scale UNIPRESS-anchored effort structurally outcompeted.
- China's rapidly scaling GaN/compound-semiconductor manufacturing base (multiple Chinese suppliers already appear in market-competitor listings) represents both a pricing threat and a geopolitical rationale for European strategic autonomy — cutting both ways for urgency and for competitive difficulty.
- EU Chips Act 2.0 and Semiconductor Coalition funding, while promising, is not yet disbursed at the scale or specificity needed for a compound-semiconductor-focused Polish program, and Poland has been noted as not having fully capitalized on the first Chips Act round.
- Risk of repeating the Ammono bankruptcy pattern if a new commercial vehicle is under-capitalized relative to the multi-year, capital-intensive path to diameter and yield competitiveness.

## 5. Strategic Recommendations for Establishing a Polish Compound-Semiconductor Ecosystem

1. **Position UNIPRESS as the materials-science and pilot-line anchor, not the sole industrial vehicle.** A realistic ecosystem design uses UNIPRESS's crystal-growth IP and pilot-scale (NL-3) operation as the seed technology and proof-of-concept supply source, while creating a separately capitalized, professionally managed manufacturing entity — jointly owned by UNIPRESS (as IP/technology contributor), Polish state investment vehicles, and private/industrial capital — to execute diameter scale-up and volume manufacturing. This avoids repeating the undercapitalized Ammono trajectory while preserving UNIPRESS's scientific leadership role.

2. **Prioritize a phased diameter-scale-up roadmap (2-inch → 4-inch → 6-inch)** explicitly tied to the market data showing 4-inch currently dominant by shipment share but 6-/8-inch growing fastest and delivering roughly 60% cost reduction per wafer versus 4-inch. Each phase should have distinct funding gates, given the capital intensity that diameter scaling requires (new autoclaves, crucibles, handling automation).

3. **Pursue EU Chips Act / Chips Joint Undertaking and Semiconductor Coalition financing explicitly for a compound-semiconductor pilot line**, distinct from and complementary to CEZAMAT's existing silicon-CMOS FAMES pilot-line role. Poland's documented under-utilization of the first Chips Act round is itself an argument for a focused, shovel-ready compound-semiconductor proposal built around UNIPRESS's existing, internationally credentialed IP base — a stronger starting position than a from-scratch initiative.

4. **Build formal linkages to CEZAMAT and Łukasiewicz-IMiF** to create a genuine substrate-to-packaged-device value chain within Poland: UNIPRESS/NL-3 for bulk crystal and substrate supply, CEZAMAT for advanced device processing and cleanroom infrastructure, IMiF for epitaxy, packaging, and testing capability, and Poland's existing back-end assets (Intel Wrocław, Amkor Kraków) as a longer-term downstream demand anchor once compound-semiconductor device flows exist domestically.

5. **Address the governance/culture gap deliberately.** Bringing in industrial operations leadership (yield engineering, supply-chain management, quality systems such as IATF 16949 or AS9100 depending on target end markets) as a distinct management layer over the new manufacturing entity, while keeping UNIPRESS's research group focused on next-generation process R&D (larger diameter growth, defect reduction, novel dopant/co-doping strategies), will let each organization operate according to its natural strengths rather than forcing an academic institute to become an industrial operator overnight.

6. **Target the premium/niche application segments first, not commodity volume competition.** Given UNIPRESS's demonstrated dislocation-density advantage, an initial commercial strategy focused on laser diodes, vertical power devices, and emerging micro-LED substrate demand — markets that specifically value low-defect-density native GaN and are less exposed to commoditized GaN-on-Si/GaN-on-SiC pricing pressure — is more defensible than attempting to compete immediately in high-volume RF/power GaN-on-SiC, where UNIPRESS has no differentiated position.

7. **Treat the Ammono bankruptcy as a design constraint, not a disqualifying precedent.** The failure appears attributable to insufficient scale/capitalization and possibly premature go-to-market timing relative to downstream European demand, rather than to any deficiency in the underlying crystal-growth science, which independent literature continues to validate as competitive. A better-capitalized, better-governed successor vehicle, launched into today's more favorable EU policy and funding environment, is not attempting the same experiment twice under the same conditions.

---

## 6. Conclusion

UNIPRESS represents one of Poland's — and one of Europe's — genuinely world-class scientific assets in compound-semiconductor materials, with a 50-year lineage of high-pressure physics expertise that produced internationally competitive, low-defect-density bulk GaN crystal growth technology and a functioning, if small-scale, commercial substrate supply operation. It is realistically evaluated as a highly credible R&D anchor and technology-IP contributor for a Polish compound-semiconductor ecosystem, but not as a self-sufficient industrial partner capable of independently building competitive wafer-manufacturing scale. The gap between UNIPRESS's proven 2-inch-class pilot capability and the 4–8 inch volume tier that defines competitiveness in the global GaN substrate market is real, well-documented in the technical literature, and cannot be closed without substantial new capital, diameter-scale-up engineering, and a governance structure distinct from the institute's traditional academic operating model. Given Poland's newly articulated national semiconductor strategy, its explicit naming of UNIPRESS as a technology-transfer node, and the availability of EU Chips Act and Semiconductor Coalition financing mechanisms, the policy and funding environment for closing this gap is more favorable now than at any point in UNIPRESS's history — making a well-structured, appropriately capitalized UNIPRESS-anchored consortium a credible, if non-trivial, path toward an advanced compound-semiconductor materials ecosystem in Poland.
