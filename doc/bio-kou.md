# Sindo Kou: A Scientific Biography

## Overview

Sindo Kou is an Emeritus Professor of Materials Science and Engineering at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, where he spent more than four decades building one of the world's most influential research programs on the metallurgy and fluid dynamics of welding, casting, and crystal growth. He is best known as the author of *Welding Metallurgy* (Wiley), now in its third edition, a text that has become a standard reference across academia and industry, and for a large body of experimental and analytical work explaining how molten-pool fluid flow, solidification, and segregation govern the quality and cracking susceptibility of welds and cast alloys.

## Early Life and Education

Kou earned a Bachelor of Science in Chemical Engineering from National Taiwan University in Taipei, Taiwan, in 1971. He then moved to the United States, earning a Master of Science in Materials Engineering from the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee in 1974. He completed his Doctor of Philosophy in Materials Science and Engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) in 1978, working in the tradition of physical metallurgy and solidification science associated with that department.

## Research Program

Kou's research career centers on **materials processing**, with three interlinked focus areas:

### 1. Welding Metallurgy and Weld-Pool Fluid Dynamics
The dominant thread of his career, spanning heat flow during welding, Marangoni (surface-tension-driven) convection in weld pools, weld penetration shape, grain structure and grain refinement in weld metal, and — most centrally in his later career — **solidification cracking (hot cracking) and liquation cracking**. Over roughly two decades (2015 onward), Kou and collaborators developed a systematic, largely analytical framework for predicting cracking susceptibility during solidification, including:
- A general criterion for cracking during solidification based on strain and permeability of the mushy zone (2015).
- Analytical and CALPHAD-based models for predicting cracking susceptibility of binary, ternary, and quaternary aluminum alloys, magnesium alloys, stainless steels, nickel-base alloys, and high-entropy alloys.
- The role of back diffusion, intergranular liquid feeding, undercooling, grain refining, and dihedral angle in either promoting or resisting cracking.
- Weldability tests (transverse-motion and other techniques) for quantifying cracking susceptibility of carbon steels, stainless steels, and aluminum alloys.
- Work extending these cracking frameworks from conventional welding into **additive manufacturing**, including a modified weldability test adapted specifically for AM deposits (2025).

### 2. Casting
Work on hot tearing and solidification cracking in cast magnesium alloys (notably ternary Mg–Al–Ca and Mg–Al–Sr systems), nanoparticle-reinforced light-alloy castings (e.g., nanoparticle-induced hot-tearing resistance in A206 aluminum alloy), and Mg-based nanocomposites (Mg-Zn/SiC systems).

### 3. Crystal Growth
Earlier in his career (roughly the late 1980s through the 2000s), Kou conducted extensive work on melt-growth crystal processes, including:
- Czochralski and liquid-encapsulated Czochralski (LEC) growth of compound semiconductors such as GaAs, GaInAs, GaInSb, and InSb, with particular attention to controlling dopant/solute segregation.
- Floating-zone crystal growth, including thermocapillary (Marangoni) convection under normal gravity and microgravity conditions, and techniques using floating shapers to control crystal shape and segregation.
- Vertical and horizontal Bridgman growth, including segregation control in CdZnTe.
- Ohno continuous casting as a crystal-growth technique.

A common intellectual thread across all three domains is the coupling of **heat transfer, fluid flow (often Marangoni/buoyancy-driven convection), and solidification/segregation phenomena** — essentially applying transport-phenomena analysis to materials processing problems, whether the melt pool is a weld, a casting, or a crystal growth interface.

## Teaching and Institutional Service

At UW–Madison, Kou taught courses in materials processing, welding metallurgy, and the joining of engineering materials. He served as Department Chair of Materials Science and Engineering from 2000 to 2004, and directed a multi-university NSF Industry/University Cooperative Research Center (I/UCRC) — the Center for Integrative Materials Joining Science for Energy Applications — from 2010 to 2015, coordinating joining-science research across UW–Madison, the Colorado School of Mines, Lehigh University, and Ohio State University with industry partners in the energy sector.

## Scientific Legacy

Kou's cumulative contribution is best understood as building a coherent, physically grounded framework connecting **transport phenomena (heat and fluid flow) to microstructure and defect formation** across three related materials-processing operations — welding, casting, and crystal growth — all of which involve a moving or stationary melt pool with a solidifying interface. Within welding specifically, his group's decades of work on weld-pool Marangoni convection helped establish the mechanistic understanding of why weld penetration shape varies with trace surface-active elements (e.g., sulfur in steel), while his more recent analytical and CALPHAD-based cracking-susceptibility models have provided practicing engineers and alloy designers with predictive tools for solidification and liquation cracking — a persistent, costly defect in both traditional welding and modern additive manufacturing. His textbook *Welding Metallurgy* remains, by citation count and continued adoption, the most widely used graduate-level reference in its field.
