# CIC nanoGUNE and the Development of Compound Semiconductor Crystal Growth and Wafer Technologies: An Assessment of Its Fit as a Partner for a Polish Semiconductor Ecosystem

---

## Executive Summary

CIC nanoGUNE (Cooperative Research Center on Nanoscience, San Sebastián, Basque Country, Spain) is a nanoscience institute with strong, internationally recognized research programs in nanooptics, quantum nanoscience, magnetism/spintronics, and nanomaterials, supported by a ~300 m² ISO 5–7 cleanroom oriented toward electron-beam lithography and device-scale nanofabrication. **It is not, and has never positioned itself as, a bulk crystal growth or wafer manufacturing institution.** It does not operate boule-growth reactors (Czochralski, Bridgman, HVPE, ammonothermal, PVT/sublimation), does not produce ingots, and does not perform wafer slicing, lapping, polishing, or epi-ready surface preparation at any relevant scale. Searches of nanoGUNE's own publication record, facility pages, and patent activity confirm this: its crystal-growth-adjacent work is confined to thin-film epitaxy (MBE-type growth of nanoscale layers and van der Waals heterostructures) and small-scale/analog "crystal growth" studies (e.g., protein deposition modeling), not compound-semiconductor bulk crystal or wafer technology in the industrial sense.

Where nanoGUNE *does* connect meaningfully to bulk crystal growth is through its role as an **advanced international partner** in the EU-funded **ENSEMBLE3 Centre of Excellence** in Warsaw — a Teaming-for-Excellence project explicitly built around Poland's own crystal-growth expertise (rooted in the legacy of Jan Czochralski) at the Łukasiewicz–Institute of Electronic Materials Technology (ITME) and the University of Warsaw, with nanoGUNE, the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, and Sapienza University of Rome contributing complementary excellence in nanophotonics, metamaterials, and characterization science rather than growth technology itself.

**Conclusion for Polish strategic planning:** CIC nanoGUNE is a credible, EU-proven partner for **upstream nanoscience, characterization, and device-physics collaboration** layered on top of Polish-grown compound semiconductor material — and it has an existing institutional relationship (via ENSEMBLE3) that could be extended. It is **not a substitute for, and cannot anchor,** the industrial crystal-growth and wafer-fabrication capability (GaN, SiC, GaAs, InP boule growth; epiready wafer production at scale) that a Polish compound-semiconductor ecosystem would need. That capability already exists domestically at ITME/Łukasiewicz Research Network and the Institute of High Pressure Physics PAS ("Unipress"/Ammono legacy), and would need to be scaled via industrial partners (e.g., established GaN/SiC boule and epiwafer producers), not via nanoGUNE.

## 1. Introduction and Scope

Poland has articulated a national semiconductor strategy ("Poland in the Game for the Future — Policy for the Semiconductor Sector 2025+") targeting specialized regional parks (Mazovia, Lower Silesia, Pomerania), international investment (e.g., Intel's Wrocław assembly/test facility, Taiwan-Poland cooperation in Pomerania), and leveraging areas of existing Polish strength — notably crystal growth, where Poland has a genuine and distinguished lineage traceable to Jan Czochralski's invention of the crystal-pulling method. Any credible plan to build an advanced compound-semiconductor crystal growth and wafer ecosystem must therefore evaluate potential partners against the specific, capital- and know-how-intensive requirements of that value chain: bulk single-crystal growth, boule processing, wafer slicing/polishing, and epitaxial-quality surface preparation for materials such as GaN, SiC, GaAs, InP, GaSb, and related alloys.

This report evaluates CIC nanoGUNE specifically against that yardstick.

## 2. What CIC nanoGUNE Actually Is

### 2.1 Institutional profile

CIC nanoGUNE is a non-profit Cooperative Research Center ("Centro de Investigación Cooperativa," CIC) established in 2009 (originating from a 2006 initiative) by the Basque Government, operating within the Basque Science, Technology and Innovation Network and closely tied to Ikerbasque, the Basque Foundation for Science. Its explicit mission is to perform "world-class nanoscience research for the competitive growth of the Basque Country," with three core pillars: Quantum Nanoscience, Nanomaterials, and Nanomedicine.

### 2.2 Research programs

nanoGUNE's demonstrated strengths, drawn from its own project and publication listings, cluster around:

- **Nanooptics and polaritonics** — near-field infrared/THz nanoscopy, phonon- and plasmon-polaritons in van der Waals and polar crystals, ultrastrong light–matter coupling, and squeezed THz waves in thin semiconductor crystals.
- **Spintronics and quantum hardware** — spin Hall effects in graphene/TMD heterostructures, epitaxial Heusler-alloy magnonics, spin-qubit readout sensors (collaboration with Quantum Motion).
- **Nanomaterials and functional coatings** — DNA-based data storage in polymer fibers (TextaDNA/EIC Pathfinder), 2D materials, and van der Waals heterostructure engineering.
- **Nanomedicine** — clinically motivated diagnostic and biosensing technologies developed with the Basque Health System (Osakidetza) and IIS Biogipuzkoa.

These are internationally excellent research areas (nanoGUNE researchers hold ERC Advanced Grants and publish in Nature Communications, Nano Letters, and comparable venues), but they are **device- and phenomenon-scale nanoscience**, not bulk materials production.

### 2.3 Facilities: cleanroom and thin-film growth, not boule growth

nanoGUNE's cleanroom is documented at approximately 300 m², classified ISO 5 (class 100) / ISO 6 (class 1000) / ISO 7 (class 10,000), with an acoustically damped bay for electron-beam lithography. This is a **device nanofabrication cleanroom** — appropriately sized and equipped for patterning, thin-film deposition, and characterization of nanoscale device structures, not for the reactor infrastructure (multi-hundred-kilogram HVPE/ammonothermal autoclaves, high-pressure solution-growth vessels, Czochralski pullers, PVT/sublimation furnaces) that define bulk compound-semiconductor crystal growth. Where nanoGUNE researchers grow crystalline material, the relevant technique class is **molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) or comparable thin-film epitaxy** for nanoscale single-crystal films and heterostructures (evidenced, for example, by MBE-grown epitaxial Heusler-alloy films in recent nanoGUNE-affiliated work) — a fundamentally different technology domain from bulk boule growth, even though both are loosely described as "crystal growth." One nanoGUNE-affiliated 2019 publication is literally titled *"A Probabilistic Model for Crystal Growth Applied to Protein Deposition at the Microscale"* — illustrative of how far the institute's actual "crystal growth" competence sits from semiconductor wafer manufacturing.

### 2.4 What nanoGUNE does not do

No evidence exists in nanoGUNE's own project descriptions, publication record, patent filings, or facility documentation of:
- Bulk single-crystal (boule) growth of GaN, SiC, GaAs, InP, GaSb, or other compound semiconductors by HVPE, ammonothermal, high-nitrogen-pressure solution growth (HNPSG), physical vapor transport (PVT), or Czochralski/Bridgman methods;
- Wafer slicing, lapping, chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP), or epi-ready surface finishing at production or even substantial pilot scale;
- Ingot-to-wafer process engineering, wafer metrology standardization, or defect-density/dislocation-density qualification protocols of the kind central to commercial substrate suppliers;
- Industrial-scale MOCVD/MBE epiwafer production lines (as distinct from research-scale thin-film growth for device physics experiments).

This is a structural, not incidental, gap: nanoGUNE was never chartered or resourced to be a materials-production institute, and its budget, headcount, and facility footprint are consistent with a focused nanoscience research center rather than a compound-semiconductor materials/wafer fab.

## 3. The Actual Link Between nanoGUNE and Crystal Growth: ENSEMBLE3

The one substantive, verifiable connection between CIC nanoGUNE and compound-semiconductor-relevant crystal growth is nanoGUNE's role as an international "advanced partner" in **ENSEMBLE3 — Centre of ExcelleNce for nanophotonicS, advancEd Materials and novel crystal growth-Based technoLogiEs**, based in Warsaw.

### 3.1 Structure of ENSEMBLE3

ENSEMBLE3 was established under the EU Horizon 2020 "Teaming for Excellence" instrument and the Foundation for Polish Science's International Research Agendas (MAB) programme (Grant Agreement No. 857543 for Teaming; MAB/2020/14 for the Polish co-financing), with a symbolic signing in Brussels in September 2019 by project coordinator Prof. Dorota A. Pawlak (Łukasiewicz–Institute of Electronic Materials Technology and University of Warsaw) and Prof. José M. Pitarke, then Director of nanoGUNE. The Centre has secured roughly €15M from the EU and €5M from Polish national authorities (through September 2026).

The consortium's stated division of expertise makes the roles explicit:
- **Polish core institutions** — the Łukasiewicz–Institute of Electronic Materials Technology (ITME) and the University of Warsaw — contribute the actual **crystal growth and materials innovation practice**, building directly on Poland's Czochralski legacy, plus a technology-transfer channel via the Warsaw-based SME 3C Crystals sp. z o.o.
- **International advanced partners** — Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (structured-light/3D nanophotonics), Sapienza University of Rome (linear/nonlinear optics theory and experiment), and **CIC nanoGUNE** (Spain) — contribute complementary scientific excellence in nanophotonics and materials physics, not crystal-growth process technology.

In other words, within the one major program that ties nanoGUNE to "crystal growth" in its very name, **the crystal growth capability is Polish**, and nanoGUNE's contribution is nanophotonics/nanooptics science that can be applied to characterize or functionalize the materials Poland already grows.

### 3.2 Implication

This existing relationship is a genuine institutional asset: Poland does not need to build a relationship with nanoGUNE from scratch, and there is precedent for structuring nanoGUNE's role correctly — as a science/characterization partner riding on top of Polish-grown crystals, not as a co-developer of growth processes. Any new bilateral collaboration should be modeled on this template rather than reassigned to a role nanoGUNE has no demonstrated capability to fill.

## 4. The Existing Polish Crystal-Growth and Wafer Ecosystem (Context for Partner-Fit Assessment)

To assess what a "compound semiconductor crystal growth and wafer manufacturing ecosystem" in Poland actually requires, and where the real gaps are, it is necessary to summarize the domestic base it would build on:

- **Institute of High Pressure Physics, Polish Academy of Sciences ("Unipress")** — pioneer of high-nitrogen-pressure solution growth (HNPSG) of bulk GaN crystals and originator of the Ammono ammonothermal GaN technology; decades of publication and patent record in bulk GaN substrates for electronic and optoelectronic devices.
- **Łukasiewicz–Institute of Electronic Materials Technology (ITME), Warsaw** — over 50 years of compound-semiconductor materials and device work spanning HgCdTe, GaAs, GaP, InP, GaSb, SiC and GaN, and the Polish anchor institution of ENSEMBLE3; also hosts HVPE and ammonothermal GaN bulk-growth activity reported in the wider Polish literature.
- **TopGaN** — GaN-based light-emitter company operating since 2001, spun from Polish GaN crystal-growth research.
- **VIGO Photonics** — established Polish compound-semiconductor (infrared detector) manufacturer actively shaping national semiconductor-sector policy input.
- **3C Crystals sp. z o.o.** — the SME technology-transfer vehicle embedded in ENSEMBLE3.
- **CEZAMAT (Warsaw University of Technology)** — nanotechnology/photonics fabrication and characterization infrastructure increasingly used as a convening venue for Polish-EU semiconductor and defense-photonics events.

Poland's declared national strategy ("Poland in the Game for the Future — Policy for the Semiconductor Sector 2025+") explicitly proposes specialized regional parks (Mazovia, Lower Silesia, Pomerania) with competence centers and university linkages, plus international investment (Intel's ~$4.6B Wrocław assembly/test facility; a Pomerania–Taiwan cooperation framework targeting a >550-hectare industrial park). Industry voices (e.g., VIGO Photonics leadership) are pressing for tighter integration of currently fragmented domestic supply chains and stronger competence-center models — a call that argues for deepening existing domestic growth capability and forging targeted materials/device partnerships, not for importing bulk crystal-growth know-how nanoGUNE does not possess.

## 5. Economic and Strategic Assessment

### 5.1 Where nanoGUNE adds real value

| Capability sought | nanoGUNE fit | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Advanced characterization (near-field IR/THz nanoscopy, polariton physics) of grown compound-semiconductor material | **Strong** | Demonstrated world-class publication record; directly applicable to defect/quality characterization and novel photonic device concepts on top of Polish-grown substrates |
| Nanoscale device fabrication/proof-of-concept on small samples | **Strong (bounded by scale)** | 300 m² ISO 5–7 cleanroom with e-beam lithography; appropriate for prototyping, not production |
| Spintronic/quantum-hardware device physics using compound-semiconductor heterostructures | **Strong** | Existing ERC-grant-caliber programs; complementary to III-V/III-N material platforms |
| Consortium/EU-funding credibility and track record | **Strong** | Proven Teaming-for-Excellence partner (ENSEMBLE3), Graphene Flagship participant, ERC grantee host |
| Bulk single-crystal (boule) growth process development (HVPE, ammonothermal, PVT, Czochralski/Bridgman) | **Absent** | No documented facilities, patents, or publication record in this domain |
| Wafer slicing, polishing, epi-ready surface engineering at pilot or production scale | **Absent** | No documented capability |
| Industrial-scale MOCVD/MBE epiwafer manufacturing | **Absent** | Facility scale and mission are research-, not production-, oriented |
| Defect/dislocation-density qualification and substrate standardization for device manufacturers | **Absent to limited** | Not part of nanoGUNE's institutional remit |

### 5.2 Economic reasoning

Bulk compound-semiconductor crystal growth and wafer manufacturing are capital-intensive, yield-driven industrial processes (autoclaves, HVPE reactors, precision slicing/polishing lines, metrology at production throughput) whose competitive advantage comes from decades of proprietary process know-how, reactor engineering, and yield learning curves — the domain of specialized industrial players (e.g., established SiC/GaN substrate manufacturers) and mission-oriented national institutes (Unipress, ITME), not of a nanoscience research center whose comparative advantage lies in fundamental physics and small-scale device demonstration. Attempting to route ecosystem-building investment through nanoGUNE for growth/wafer capability would misallocate capital against nanoGUNE's actual cost structure and expertise curve, and would duplicate capability Poland already possesses domestically at Unipress and ITME. Conversely, treating nanoGUNE as a science/characterization and co-innovation partner — exactly the role it already plays in ENSEMBLE3 — captures real value at low incremental cost, since the institutional relationship, EU funding mechanisms, and trust already exist.

### 5.3 Recommended partnership architecture

1. **Do not position nanoGUNE as a crystal-growth or wafer-manufacturing partner.** Its facilities and mission preclude this role.
2. **Extend the ENSEMBLE3 template** for any new Poland-nanoGUNE collaboration: Polish institutions (ITME/Łukasiewicz, Unipress, University of Warsaw) supply and grow the compound-semiconductor material; nanoGUNE contributes characterization science (near-field nanoscopy, polariton/THz spectroscopy of grown material), joint device-physics research, and small-scale nanofabrication/prototyping.
3. **Pair nanoGUNE's characterization strength with Poland's growth strength** to accelerate quality feedback loops (e.g., using nanoGUNE's THz/IR nanoscopy to characterize defect and polariton behavior in Polish-grown GaN/SiC boules or wafers) — a genuinely additive, low-duplication use of nanoGUNE's actual expertise.
4. **Source the industrial wafer-manufacturing scale-up (production HVPE/ammonothermal reactors, polishing lines, epiwafer capacity) from dedicated industrial or state-institute investment** (Unipress, ITME, and/or an international compound-semiconductor manufacturer as an anchor investor/JV partner), consistent with the national strategy's own emphasis on specialized regional parks and competence centers.
5. **Use nanoGUNE's EU-grant track record** (Teaming for Excellence, Graphene Flagship, ERC hosting) as a template for structuring future Poland-led Horizon Europe or EU Chips Act-adjacent consortium bids that combine Polish growth capability with nanoGUNE-style characterization/photonics excellence.

## 6. Conclusion

CIC nanoGUNE is an internationally excellent nanoscience institute whose core competencies — nanooptics, polaritonics, spintronics, nanoscale device fabrication, and nanomedicine — are genuinely valuable but structurally distinct from bulk compound-semiconductor crystal growth and wafer manufacturing. Its single documented link to "crystal growth" is as a nanophotonics-and-materials-science partner within Poland's own ENSEMBLE3 Centre of Excellence, where the actual crystal-growth expertise is supplied by Polish institutions (ITME/Łukasiewicz, building on the Czochralski legacy) rather than by nanoGUNE itself. For Poland's stated ambition of an advanced compound-semiconductor crystal-growth and wafer-manufacturing ecosystem, nanoGUNE should be engaged — via and beyond ENSEMBLE3 — as a science and characterization partner that adds value on top of domestically grown material, while the industrial crystal-growth and wafer-production capability should continue to be built from Poland's existing strengths (Unipress, ITME, TopGaN, VIGO Photonics) and from targeted industrial investment, not from nanoGUNE.
